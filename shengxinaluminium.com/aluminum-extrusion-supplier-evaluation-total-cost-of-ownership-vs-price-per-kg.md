# Aluminum Extrusion Supplier Evaluation: Total Cost of Ownership vs. Price Per Kg

## The Real Unit of Cost Is an Accepted Part

Most aluminum extrusion buying decisions begin with the most convenient number: dollars per kilogram. The figure looks objective, precise, and easy to place in a spreadsheet. It is also a price for a supplier-defined bundle of assumptions.

Your assembly does not consume kilograms. It consumes parts with the correct alloy, temper, dimensions, finish, cut length, packaging, and delivery timing. Production accepts usable parts, not the weight of material that passed through an extrusion press. That makes the meaningful purchasing question much more specific:

**What does one conforming, production-ready part cost when it reaches the point of use?**

A disciplined [supplier evaluation framework](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/how-to-evaluate-aluminum-extrusion-suppliers-beyond-price-per-kg_n1043) starts by translating every quote into that same economic unit. Price per kg still has value. It helps separate metal cost from conversion cost and reveals changes in yield or process efficiency. It should remain an input to the decision, not the decision itself.

## Why Price Per Kg Creates False Equivalence

### A kilogram does not guarantee the same output

An extrusion quote may use any of several weight definitions:

- Theoretical weight calculated from the drawing
- Actual weight produced at the press
- Weight shipped in bundles
- Weight after cutting and machining
- Weight that passes dimensional and cosmetic inspection

Those definitions are not interchangeable. Extrusion creates setup scrap, billet butts, run-out, saw kerf, short lengths, and occasional dimensional or surface rejects. A supplier with better usable yield can quote a higher conversion rate and still deliver a lower cost per accepted part.

Consider a profile that weighs 0.75 kg per meter and is cut into 2.4-meter pieces. Each good piece weighs approximately 1.8 kg. An order for 10,000 accepted pieces requires 18,000 kg of usable material.

- At a 96% usable yield, production requires about 18,750 kg.
- At a 99% usable yield, production requires about 18,182 kg.
- The difference is roughly 568 kg before the cost of finishing, handling, and inspection.

If the finished material represents about $5 per kg of economic value, that yield difference is worth approximately $2,840. It is already comparable to a $0.15-per-kg price advantage on the 18,000 kg of accepted product. The lower quote has not necessarily produced a lower cost.

### The same price can cover different scopes

Two suppliers can quote the same profile at $4.20 per kg while offering materially different deliverables. One price may include only mill-finish extrusion. The other may include aging, sawing, anodizing, protective packaging, inspection records, and delivery.

A comparable quote needs an explicit boundary around every operation:

- Alloy, temper, and billet or metal pricing basis
- Extrusion conversion
- Die and tooling charges
- Straightening and aging
- Cut-to-length work
- Anodizing or powder coating
- CNC machining or fabrication
- Inspection and documentation
- Packaging, freight, duties, and insurance

A lower price per kg often means that one of these costs has been excluded or transferred to the buyer. The number is not false; it is simply incomplete.

### A snapshot price can hide a different metal formula

Aluminum material is frequently tied to an index or regional premium. One supplier may quote using a current reference price, while another uses a monthly average or a different adjustment date. One may show metal and conversion separately; another may combine them into a single number.

For a fair comparison, identify the following:

- The aluminum reference price and whether it uses a cash or three-month basis
- The regional physical premium
- The pricing date or averaging period
- The frequency and formula for future adjustments
- The fixed conversion premium
- The weight basis used for billing

A $0.20-per-kg difference may reflect nothing more than different metal assumptions. It should not be treated as evidence that one supplier operates more efficiently until the formulas are aligned.

## Build a Cost-per-Accepted-Part Model

The practical version of total cost of ownership is simple:

**TCO per accepted part = all-in program cost ÷ conforming parts received**

All-in program cost includes supplier invoices and the buyer's predictable internal costs. Depending on the project, the cost stack may contain:

- Material and extrusion conversion
- Tooling and die maintenance
- Finishing and machining
- Freight, duties, and packaging
- Inventory and working capital
- Incoming inspection and sorting
- Rework, scrap, and replacement logistics
- Production disruption caused by late or nonconforming material

Before adding a cost, mark it as included, excluded, or uncertain in the supplier's quote. The model is useful only when each item appears once.

### Freeze the comparison basis first

Send every shortlisted supplier the same commercial and technical assumptions. At minimum, define:

- Drawing revision and cross-section weight
- Alloy and temper
- Dimensional, straightness, and twist tolerances
- Visible surfaces and finish requirements
- Cut length and length tolerance
- Machining or fabrication requirements
- Annual volume and release quantity
- Packaging and protection requirements
- Delivery location and Incoterm
- Inspection records and material documentation
- Metal index, adjustment method, and quote validity

If one supplier quotes a yearly volume and another quotes a small release, the prices are not comparable. If one supplier assumes mill finish and another assumes anodizing, averaging the two numbers only conceals the difference.

### Normalize the quote to good output

When the quote is based on production or shipped weight, use a yield adjustment:

`accepted output = production quantity × usable yield`

`normalized extrusion cost = quoted extrusion spend ÷ accepted output`

The commercial rule matters. If a supplier bills only accepted weight, replaces rejected material at no charge, or absorbs process scrap, use that actual rule instead. Do not apply a yield adjustment twice. Even when replacement material is free, retain the cost of inspection, sorting, schedule delay, and extra handling.

For cut or machined profiles, calculate cost per conforming piece whenever possible. A kilogram is a reasonable denominator for commodity material. It becomes less useful as the product accumulates operations priced by surface area, cycle time, color batch, setup, or piece count.

## Worked Example: The Higher Quote That Costs Less

Assume an OEM needs 12,000 accepted kg per year of a 6063-T5 profile that is anodized and cut before delivery. Both hypothetical suppliers meet the drawing requirements. Their extrusion prices are quoted on the same produced-weight basis, and the other figures represent normalized buyer costs.

### Supplier A: lower extrusion quote

- Mill-finish extrusion: $4.20 per kg
- Usable yield: 96%
- Extrusion cost after yield adjustment: $4.20 ÷ 0.96 = $4.38 per accepted kg
- Anodizing: $0.78 per kg
- Freight and duties: $0.42 per kg
- Die amortization: $0.12 per kg
- Additional inventory carrying cost: $0.18 per kg
- Expected inspection, rework, and quality cost: $0.30 per kg
- **Total normalized cost: approximately $6.18 per accepted kg**

### Supplier B: higher extrusion quote

- Mill-finish extrusion: $4.50 per kg
- Usable yield: 99%
- Extrusion cost after yield adjustment: $4.50 ÷ 0.99 = $4.55 per accepted kg
- Anodizing: $0.55 per kg
- Freight and duties: $0.18 per kg
- Die amortization: $0.08 per kg
- Additional inventory carrying cost: $0.05 per kg
- Expected inspection, rework, and quality cost: $0.08 per kg
- **Total normalized cost: approximately $5.49 per accepted kg**

Supplier A appears to win the first comparison by $0.30 per kg. After yield adjustment, its extrusion advantage is only about $0.17 per accepted kg. Supplier B saves $0.86 per kg across finishing, logistics, tooling, inventory, and quality. At 12,000 accepted kg per year, Supplier B's estimated advantage is:

`12,000 × ($6.18 − $5.49) = $8,280 per year`

If each finished piece weighs 1.8 kg, the difference is approximately $1.24 per accepted piece. The higher conversion quote is not a premium in economic terms; it is the price of a process that creates more usable output and fewer downstream costs.

The figures are illustrative, not a market benchmark. The important practice is to expose the assumptions and replace estimates with measured results from a production trial.

## The Cost Sources Most Often Missed

### Yield turns scrap into a denominator problem

A supplier may discuss scrap as a normal manufacturing expense, while the buyer experiences it as fewer parts, extra material, or a late shipment. Ask how the supplier treats setup scrap, billet ends, saw kerf, short lengths, rejected finish, and rework. Also ask whether the yield estimate comes from comparable profiles or from a generic allowance.

A complex hollow profile with thin walls may have a different yield profile from a simple solid bar, even when both are priced per kilogram. The usable-output calculation must match the actual geometry and finishing route.

### Tooling is a volume problem

A $6,000 die has a very different economic effect at different volumes:

- At 2,000 good kg, it adds $3.00 per kg.
- At 60,000 good kg, it adds $0.10 per kg.

Use expected cumulative good volume, not an optimistic forecast, when amortizing the die. Keep the cash-flow question separate as well. A supplier may offer a low per-kg price but require a large upfront tooling payment. Confirm who owns the die, who pays for correction or replacement, and whether the tool can move if the program changes suppliers.

### Finishing and machining are not naturally weight-based

Anodizing cost depends heavily on surface area, alloy response, color, masking, rack arrangement, and batch size. Powder coating adds color-change and thickness considerations. CNC machining is driven by cycle time, fixturing, tool wear, and setup complexity. None of those costs necessarily declines in proportion to profile weight.

A surface defect discovered after anodizing can waste the value of the extrusion, the finishing operation, the handling, and the scheduled production slot. For cosmetic profiles, compare first-pass acceptance and cost per finished piece rather than treating coating as a small per-kilogram add-on.

### Freight is a route problem, not just a weight problem

Long aluminum profiles can fill a truck or container by length and volume before they reach the weight limit. Protective packaging, consolidation, transloading, and damage claims can change the landed cost substantially. An ex-works quote should never be compared directly with a delivered quote.

Normalize freight, insurance, duties, customs charges, and packaging to the same delivery point. If the supplier is overseas, include the inventory needed to cover ocean transit and customs variability. A factory price advantage that disappears after delivery is not a sourcing advantage.

### Quality is an expected-value problem

Quality cost includes more than rejected material. It can include incoming inspection, sorting labor, rework, expedited replacement, line downtime, customer containment, and administrative effort. A supplier may replace defective parts without charging for them, yet the buyer can still absorb most of the disruption.

Expected quality cost can be modeled as:

`defect probability × consequence per event + routine inspection and rework cost`

Suppose a program receives 20 shipments per year and one serious defect event typically costs $12,000 in sorting, downtime, and expedited replacement. The expected event cost is $600 per shipment. If each shipment contains 2,000 kg, that is approximately $0.30 per kg before routine inspection. A supplier that is $0.25 per kg cheaper can lose that advantage through a single recurring failure pattern.

### Lead time creates an inventory charge

A supplier with inconsistent lead times often causes the buyer to carry safety stock. For a program consuming 60,000 kg per year, six additional weeks of inventory equals approximately 6,923 kg. At a delivered value of $5 per kg, that inventory ties up about $34,615. With a 20% annual carrying rate, the extra cost is approximately $6,923 per year, before considering obsolescence or damage.

The quoted unit price does not show that cost. A reliable supplier can therefore be cheaper even when its invoice price is higher.

## Ask for Quotes That Reveal the Real Cost

A useful RFQ does not ask only for a price per kg. It asks suppliers to expose the inputs that determine the price and the buyer's risk. Require each quote to identify:

- Metal price, alloy surcharge, index, and adjustment formula
- Conversion charge and the exact billing weight
- Yield or scrap assumptions
- Die price, ownership, expected life, and correction terms
- Finishing, machining, and inspection charges
- Packaging, freight, duties, insurance, and delivery terms
- Minimum order quantity and price breaks by release size
- Lead time for tooling, first production, and repeat releases
- Included documentation and quality records
- Remedies for nonconforming or late material

Where the part geometry allows it, request both a per-kilogram price and a per-finished-piece price. The supplier should state whether the piece price assumes theoretical weight, actual shipped weight, or accepted output. That single clarification can reveal why two apparently similar quotes are far apart.

Ask suppliers to price at least two or three realistic release quantities. A supplier that is attractive at a full-truck annual release may be expensive when the plant actually needs smaller monthly shipments. The quote should reflect the way the program will be purchased, not an artificial volume that will never occur.

## Use Sensitivity Analysis Before Awarding the Business

Cost estimates contain uncertainty. Yield, coating first-pass rate, freight, metal premiums, and quality events are not always known precisely before production starts. A single spreadsheet total can create false confidence.

Run at least three cases:

- **Expected case:** the most supportable yield, freight, volume, and quality assumptions
- **Conservative case:** lower yield, higher finish rejects, longer inventory coverage, and higher logistics cost
- **Stress case:** a plausible disruption such as a delayed shipment, serious quality event, or large metal-premium change

A supplier should not win solely because it has the lowest expected number if a realistic downside makes the program unworkable. Conversely, a higher quote may be justified when its cost remains competitive across all three cases.

The break-even question is direct: how much additional price can a supplier charge before its savings in yield, finishing, logistics, inventory, and quality disappear? In the worked example, Supplier B has an estimated $0.69-per-kg advantage after all adjustments. That is the margin available for inaccurate assumptions. If a trial shows that its finishing or quality savings were overstated by more than that amount, the ranking changes.

## Replace Assumptions With Production Evidence

A cost model becomes credible when the supplier's process produces evidence under normal conditions. A trial or first production run should track:

- Starting material weight and accepted output weight
- Number of accepted pieces and actual piece weight
- Setup scrap, cut loss, and rejected lengths
- Dimensional and straightness rejection
- Finish first-pass acceptance and rework
- Packaging damage and transit loss
- Actual production lead time and delivery variance
- Inspection labor and corrective-action effort

The trial should use the intended die, press, aging cycle, finishing route, and packaging method. A hand-selected sample made under special conditions can make a supplier look cheaper than its normal production process really is.

Use the measured results to update the expected cost per accepted part. If the supplier's original quote depended on a 99% yield but the trial produces 96%, the difference belongs in the commercial review rather than being dismissed as an isolated event.

## The Procurement Question That Matters

Price per kg is useful for analyzing metal exposure, conversion efficiency, and negotiation leverage. It is not a reliable award criterion when suppliers differ in yield, scope, finishing, logistics, quality performance, or lead-time reliability.

The stronger decision rule is:

**Choose the supplier with the lowest risk-adjusted total cost for accepted product at the required service level.**

That may be the supplier with the lowest kilogram price. It may also be the supplier whose quote is higher because it includes better yield, integrated finishing, stronger packaging, shorter replenishment time, or more consistent quality. The deciding number is what the program pays for usable parts—not what the supplier charges for metal before the real work begins.
