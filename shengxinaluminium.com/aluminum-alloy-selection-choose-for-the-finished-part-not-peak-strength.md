# Aluminum Alloy Selection: Choose for the Finished Part, Not Peak Strength

## The material at the press is not the material in service

An aluminum extrusion is usually specified as an alloy-temper pair, but the part that carries load in service may be bent, welded, machined, drilled, anodized, painted, and exposed to weather. Each step changes the balance of strength, ductility, dimensional stability, corrosion resistance, or appearance.

That leads to the most useful rule in aluminum alloy selection:

> Specify the alloy against the finished part and its final service condition, not against the material's headline strength at the press exit.

A [practical alloy guide](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-alloys-decoded-match-your-project-to-the-right-grade_n968) helps decode series and temper designations. The engineering decision starts after that lookup, when the production route and the actual failure mode are known.

## Strength is not stiffness

Peak tensile strength is easy to compare, so it often becomes the default selection criterion. For many extruded profiles, however, elastic deflection or vibration controls the design long before the metal reaches its yield strength.

The distinction matters because common wrought aluminum alloys have nearly the same modulus of elasticity. 6061 and 6063 are both close to 10,000 ksi, or about 69 GPa. For the same profile geometry, span, supports, and load, switching between those grades produces little change in elastic deflection.

Their yield strengths are different. Typical 6061-T6 values are about 40 ksi yield strength and 45 ksi tensile strength, while 6063-T6 is commonly around 31 ksi yield strength and 35 ksi tensile strength. Those differences affect the load at which permanent deformation begins, but they do not make a flexible profile suddenly rigid.

Bending stiffness is governed primarily by E × I:

- E is the material's elastic modulus, which changes relatively little among common aluminum extrusion alloys.
- I is the section's moment of inertia, which can change dramatically when the profile becomes deeper, gains a closed cavity, or receives an internal rib.

If a mullion or machine-frame rail is failing a deflection limit, increasing profile depth or improving the support arrangement may solve the problem more efficiently than moving from 6063 to 6061. If the same profile is yielding, cracking, or buckling locally, the alloy and temper become more important.

The first design question should therefore be: **What happens first?**

- Excessive deflection or vibration points toward section geometry, span, support spacing, and sometimes a stiffer profile layout.
- Yielding or fracture points toward alloy, temper, wall thickness, stress concentrations, and load path.
- Fatigue points toward stress range, surface condition, welds, holes, corners, and residual stress rather than tensile strength alone.
- Manufacturing failure points toward extrudability, bendability, weldability, die feasibility, and scrap risk.
- Corrosion or visible finish failure points toward alloy chemistry, pretreatment, coating, and exposure.

A stronger grade is useful only when strength is the limiting property.

## Why 6063 can be the better engineering choice than 6061

The 6063-versus-6061 decision shows why the finished part matters more than the strongest available grade.

6061 contains more magnesium and copper than 6063 and generally delivers higher mechanical strength in the T6 temper. 6063 contains less copper, extrudes more easily, supports more intricate thin-wall shapes, and usually produces a more uniform anodized appearance. Both can be good materials; they solve different problems.

Consider an anodized window frame or curtain-wall mullion. The profile must carry wind load, maintain a controlled deflection, hold dimensional tolerances, and present a consistent color across long visible sections. A 6063-T5 or 6063-T6 profile is often a strong candidate because:

- Its high extrudability supports complex cavities, narrow walls, and clean corners.
- Its lower copper content generally favors a more consistent anodized finish.
- Its corrosion resistance suits many architectural environments.
- The section can be enlarged or reinforced to meet stiffness requirements without paying for a difficult-to-extrude high-strength alloy.

6061 may be the better choice when the same part must carry a higher concentrated load, accept demanding machining, retain more strength around a mechanical connection, or serve as a structural component rather than a visible architectural surface. Powder coating also reduces the practical finishing difference between the two grades, giving the mechanical requirements more weight.

The mistake is treating 6061 as an automatic upgrade. If the profile is deflection-limited, 6061 may add cost without solving the governing problem. If the profile is anodized and geometry can provide the required section stiffness, 6063 may produce a lighter, cleaner, and more economical finished part.

## The last operation can reverse the grade decision

An alloy that looks ideal in an extrusion catalog can become the wrong material after the next manufacturing step. Welding and bending are especially important because they change the local temper and introduce new failure locations.

### Welding moves the design value away from parent metal strength

Heat-treated 6xxx alloys are commonly welded, but the heat-affected zone loses strength during the thermal cycle. A 6061-T6 profile may have a yield strength near 40 ksi in the unaffected parent material, while the region next to a weld can soften substantially. A reduction of roughly 30% is often used as a warning-scale estimate, but the actual value depends on alloy, thickness, welding process, joint design, cooling, and applicable design code.

The parent-metal number must not be applied uniformly across a welded frame. A sound specification identifies:

1. The alloy and temper before welding.
2. The weld process and filler material.
3. The expected softened zone.
4. The design value used for the welded joint and surrounding heat-affected area.
5. Whether post-weld heat treatment is practical.

Post-weld solution treatment and aging can restore properties in some designs, but large assemblies may distort, lose dimensional accuracy, or require complete stripping and refinishing. For many frames, the more realistic solution is to design the joint around the softened condition.

This is why 6061, 6005, or 6082 may be selected for a welded structural frame even though 6063 is easier to extrude. The final load path, not the press speed, determines the priority. A profile intended for a welded battery enclosure, transit structure, or machine frame must be evaluated as a welded assembly.

### Bending makes temper more important than the grade number

A 6061-T6 profile can be strong and difficult to bend without cracking. The same alloy in T4 condition has substantially better formability but lower immediate strength. If a design requires tight post-extrusion bends, the production sequence may be more important than the nominal alloy choice.

A workable route can involve extruding in a formable condition, performing the bend, and then aging the part to achieve the required final properties. That approach is not automatic for every alloy, shape, or bend radius. The supplier must confirm whether the final temper can be achieved without distortion, loss of dimensional control, or unacceptable surface damage.

Specifying 6061-T6 and asking a fabricator to make tight bends afterward is not a complete manufacturing plan. The drawing needs to identify when forming occurs and which properties are required after forming.

### Machining exposes stability and local-section problems

Precision machining introduces another version of the same issue. Removing material can release residual stress, and thin extruded walls can move when pockets, holes, or slots are cut. A stress-relieved temper such as T651 may be appropriate when dimensional stability matters, provided it is available for the selected alloy and product form.

The relevant question is not simply whether the alloy machines well. It is whether the machined component will remain within tolerance after material removal, fastening, coating, and service temperature changes.

## Surface finish and environment can outweigh strength

The alloy is part of the surface-treatment system. Finishing does not erase the effects of alloy chemistry; it changes which effects are visible and how much protection the part receives.

### Anodizing is chemistry-sensitive

Anodizing grows an oxide layer from the aluminum surface. Copper, zinc, manganese, iron, grain structure, extrusion history, and temper can influence how evenly that layer forms and how consistently it reflects light.

6063 is widely used for architectural anodizing because its composition and extrusion behavior support a clean, uniform appearance. 6061 can also be anodized successfully, but long visible runs may show more variation in shade or surface texture, particularly when different lots, wall thicknesses, or finishing conditions are combined.

For an anodized facade, a slightly lower-strength alloy with predictable appearance can be a better technical choice than a stronger grade that produces inconsistent color. A production sample from the actual die, alloy lot, and anodizing line is more informative than a generic finish sample.

### Powder coating broadens the candidate range

Powder coating is generally less sensitive to small alloy differences than anodizing, provided cleaning, etching, conversion coating, and curing are controlled. That gives the designer more freedom to prioritize structural properties, weld behavior, or cost.

The finish still cannot compensate for an alloy that is unsuitable for the environment. A coating defect, damaged edge, drilled hole, or poorly sealed joint can expose the underlying metal. Outdoor and industrial designs should evaluate the complete corrosion system, not just the nominal coating thickness.

### Marine exposure changes the shortlist

For saltwater structures, 5xxx alloys such as 5083 and 5086 are often considered because of their marine corrosion resistance and weldability, subject to profile availability and the governing marine standard. Copper-containing high-strength grades require more careful corrosion protection and inspection.

A 6061 profile that performs well indoors may not be the best choice for a continuously wet or salt-laden structure. The correct alloy is the one that retains acceptable properties after the expected exposure, surface damage, and maintenance conditions.

## Why 7075 is a conditional solution, not a default upgrade

7075 and other 7xxx alloys can deliver much higher strength than common 6xxx extrusion grades. That advantage is valuable when calculations prove that a lower-strength alloy cannot meet a weight, stress, fatigue, or envelope requirement.

The tradeoffs are substantial:

- Higher flow stress makes extrusion more difficult.
- Complex thin-wall profiles may require simplification or thicker sections.
- Press speeds can be lower, while die wear and scrap risk can increase.
- Corrosion protection becomes more important.
- Forming and tight bending are less forgiving.
- Welding is generally not the first-choice joining method for high-integrity 7075 structures.

A 7075 profile that cannot be extruded in the required shape, cannot be bent without cracking, or must be heavily protected against corrosion may create a worse part despite its higher tensile strength. It makes sense when the final design remains simple, the joining method is compatible, and the weight or stress calculation justifies the added manufacturing burden.

## Temper is part of the alloy selection, not a suffix added later

The alloy establishes the chemistry; the temper establishes much of the usable condition. Treating the temper as an afterthought is one of the fastest ways to invalidate an otherwise reasonable specification.

- **T4** is solution heat-treated and naturally aged. It offers better formability and is useful when the profile must be formed after extrusion.
- **T5** is artificially aged after cooling from the extrusion process. It is efficient for many architectural and general-purpose profiles because it avoids a separate full solution-treatment route.
- **T6** is solution heat-treated and artificially aged. It provides high strength but usually reduces post-extrusion formability.
- **T651** adds stress relief, typically by controlled stretching, when dimensional stability is important for machining or precision work.

T5 is not simply an inferior T6, and T6 is not automatically the correct choice. T5 may satisfy a structural requirement while offering lower processing cost and less distortion. T6 may be necessary for a calculated strength margin, but its extra heat treatment can affect straightness, tolerances, and production time.

The temper should therefore be selected with the operation sequence visible. A drawing that says only 6061 aluminum leaves too much unanswered. A drawing that says 6061-T6 before a major weld or bend may describe the wrong material condition for the finished part.

## A decision sequence that survives engineering review

A defensible aluminum alloy selection can follow six steps.

1. **Write the final service condition.** State whether the profile is welded, bent, machined, anodized, powder coated, exposed to salt, or used indoors. Include temperature, load duration, and maintenance assumptions.
2. **Identify the governing limit state.** Separate deflection, yield, fatigue, buckling, fracture, corrosion, appearance, and dimensional stability. Do not let tensile strength stand in for every requirement.
3. **Map the process route.** Mark every thermal and mechanical operation. Apply properties after welding, forming, heat treatment, and machining rather than using the incoming extrusion value everywhere.
4. **Shortlist by application logic.** Complex anodized architectural profiles often point toward 6063. Welded structural profiles may favor 6061, 6005, or 6082. Marine structures may require a 5xxx grade. Extreme strength with no welding or tight forming may justify a 7xxx grade.
5. **Check whether the geometry is manufacturable.** Confirm minimum wall thickness, hollow-section feasibility, corner radii, extrusion ratio, press capacity, tolerances, and expected scrap before approving the alloy.
6. **Validate the final part.** Use production-representative samples for mechanical testing, weld qualification, anodizing, coating, corrosion exposure, and dimensional inspection.

The shortlist should be judged on total production and service risk. A grade that is 20% stronger but requires a slower press, a more complex die, added corrosion protection, and a different joining process may be less economical than a lower-strength grade with a better profile design.

## What belongs on the drawing

A useful extrusion specification should identify more than an alloy number. It should include:

- Alloy and temper, such as 6063-T5 or 6082-T6.
- The applicable material and dimensional standard.
- Minimum mechanical properties for the relevant wall thickness.
- Whether properties apply before or after welding, bending, or heat treatment.
- Surface finish, pretreatment, color, and acceptance requirements.
- Environmental exposure and corrosion-protection expectations.
- Critical dimensions, straightness, twist, wall thickness, and bend radii.
- Required production samples or qualification tests.

For example, an anodized architectural profile might be specified around 6063-T5 with a defined anodizing class, color-matching sample, and wind-deflection requirement. A welded structural frame might specify 6061-T6 parent material but require design calculations and testing based on the post-weld heat-affected condition.

Those specifications describe the part that will actually be delivered rather than the billet that entered the press.

## The practical rule

The best extrusion alloy is not the one with the highest catalog strength. It is the grade and temper that remain adequate after extrusion, forming, joining, finishing, and environmental exposure while still producing the required shape at an acceptable cost.

Choose 6063 when extrudability and finish quality govern. Choose 6061, 6005, or 6082 when structural performance and joining carry more weight. Consider 5xxx alloys for demanding marine exposure and 7xxx alloys only when the performance calculation proves that their manufacturing and corrosion tradeoffs are justified.

Aluminum alloy selection becomes reliable when the final part—not the alloy label—is treated as the material being engineered.
