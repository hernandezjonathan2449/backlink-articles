# Aluminum Cutting Feed Rate: The Key to Clean Extrusion Cuts

## The cut is governed by chip load, not the saw’s label

Most recommendations begin with the tool: miter saw, circular saw, band saw, or CNC service. The more important control is what happens after the blade starts moving. Feed rate determines whether each tooth shears a useful chip or merely rubs against the aluminum.

A broader [aluminum cutting comparison](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/best-way-to-cut-aluminum-extrusion-several-methods-ranked-by-clean-results_n1026) can help match a tool to a project, but the ranking only becomes meaningful when the tool allows a stable feed. A premium miter saw still produces gummed teeth if the operator creeps through the cut. A modest band saw can outperform it on thin-wall tubing when its feed pressure stays controlled.

Feed rate means the speed at which the workpiece advances into the cutting teeth. On a miter saw, it is the rate of the blade’s descent. On a circular saw, it is the forward travel of the saw through the stock. On a band saw, it is usually controlled by downfeed pressure or a hydraulic mechanism. CNC equipment applies the same principle through programmed movement.

For a circular blade, a useful approximation is:

Chip load ≈ linear feed rate ÷ (RPM × tooth count)

That relationship explains why blade specifications and hand technique cannot be separated. At the same feed and RPM, a 100-tooth blade takes a smaller bite per tooth than an 80-tooth blade. The smaller bite may improve the finish on thin-wall profiles, but if the operator also slows down excessively, the teeth can polish the aluminum instead of cutting it.

As a practical example, an 80-tooth blade turning at 3,800 RPM presents 304,000 tooth engagements per minute. If the extrusion barely advances, many of those teeth are rubbing over the same surface. The resulting heat and adhesion can be worse than with a faster, more confident feed.

## Why a timid feed gums the blade

Aluminum feels soft under a blade, so slowing down seems like the safest approach. That intuition is often wrong. A tooth must penetrate far enough to form a chip. When the feed is too light, the tooth slides across the surface, compresses the material, and generates friction without clearing enough material from the cut.

The localized interface can become hot even though aluminum conducts heat well. Heat may spread through the profile, but the tiny contact area at the tooth edge still experiences pressure and friction. Aluminum then adheres to the carbide in a built-up edge. The blade appears dull even when the carbide is still intact.

The typical signs of an underfed cut include:

- A polished or smeared cut surface instead of distinct chip marks
- Gray or silver material welded into the blade gullets
- A high-pitched squeal that continues even with a sharp blade
- A cut that becomes rougher after several pieces
- Increasing resistance at the same feed setting
- Burrs that look torn rather than cleanly sheared

Gumming creates a feedback loop. The buildup changes the tooth geometry, reduces the available gullet space, and increases friction. The operator then slows down because the saw feels harder to push, which creates even more heat and buildup.

The correct response is not to force the blade. First verify that the blade is intended for nonferrous metal and has suitable carbide geometry, such as a triple-chip grind design for many circular and miter saw applications. Then confirm that the gullets are clear and the blade is sharp. If the setup is correct, a small increase in steady feed is often more effective than a timid, stop-and-start motion.

A lower blade speed can also reduce heat, but it changes chip load. RPM and feed must be adjusted as a pair. Use the blade and machine manufacturer’s recommendations rather than treating a lower RPM as a universal cure. Apply only manufacturer-approved lubricant, and never press a wax stick, rag, or other object against a moving blade.

## Why forcing the feed creates a different failure

The opposite mistake is treating aluminum like a material that should be pushed through as quickly as possible. A high feed increases chip load. Once the tooth is taking more material than the blade, motor, and workholding can support, the cut begins to degrade quickly.

The first warning is usually a change in sound. The motor bogs, the blade pitch drops, or the saw vibrates as the tooth enters a thick web. The blade may deflect sideways, producing an out-of-square cut even though the fence is correctly calibrated. A handheld circular saw can twist against its guide, increasing the risk of binding or kickback.

Excessive feed commonly produces:

- Large, irregular burrs on the exit side
- Chipped corners on anodized or thin-wall profiles
- Heavy tooth marks and a rough cut face
- Blade deflection and angled cuts
- Crushed or distorted hollow sections
- Sudden grabbing when a tooth catches a thick internal web

The best feed is therefore neither the slowest nor the fastest. It is the lightest continuous pressure that allows the blade to produce real chips without bogging, wandering, or deforming the profile.

## Profile geometry determines the usable feed window

Alloy affects cutting behavior, but profile geometry often has a greater effect on feed control. A solid bar, a multi-cavity T-slot extrusion, and a 1/16-inch wall tube may all be made from aluminum, yet the correct pressure and cutting action are very different.

### Solid and heavy-wall profiles

Solid stock absorbs vibration and gives the teeth consistent support, but it places a greater load on the blade. A feed that works on a thin rectangular tube may overload the motor in a solid bar. The right response to motor bogging is to reduce feed pressure slightly and inspect the blade, not to push harder in an attempt to finish faster.

6061 aluminum generally resists deformation more than softer 6063, but it can place more load on the cutting edge. 6063 profiles may cut easily while being more prone to smearing and adhesion if the blade becomes hot. These differences change the tuning window; they do not create a universal feed setting.

### T-slot and multi-wall extrusions

T-slot profiles create changing tooth engagement. A blade may pass through a thin outer wall, an empty cavity, and then a thick internal web within the same cut. The operator often feels this as alternating resistance.

The solution is a stable feed rather than a series of sudden corrections. Keep the extrusion firmly against the fence, clamp it close to the cut, and maintain a controlled descent or forward motion. Accelerating through a web can cause chipping; pausing at the web can cause rubbing and heat. The blade should continue moving at a pace the motor can sustain.

### Thin-wall tubing

Thin-wall tubing requires low force, but it does not benefit from a stalled blade. That distinction matters. Excessive feed pressure can crush or buckle the wall, while an almost motionless blade can rub the same edge until it becomes hot and ragged.

A band saw often gives the operator a better feed-control range because its continuous blade applies less impact than a circular blade. A fine-pitch bi-metal blade, often in the 14-to-18-teeth-per-inch range for delicate sections, can keep several teeth engaged at once when matched correctly to the wall thickness. The exact pitch should follow the blade manufacturer’s guidance.

If a thin tube collapses, reduce feed force and improve support before changing speed. A properly fitted internal support, sacrificial insert, or closer clamping arrangement may solve a problem that no feed adjustment can correct. If the tube remains intact but the edge is smeared, the blade may be underfed or contaminated with aluminum buildup.

## How feed rate behaves on different saws

**Miter saws:** Feed is controlled by the descent of the saw head. Start with the blade at operating speed, enter the profile smoothly, and maintain consistent downward pressure. Avoid dropping the head rapidly through the first wall and then stopping at an internal web. Keep the workpiece clamped against the fence and support the offcut so the exit side does not tear away.

**Circular saws:** Feed stability depends on the entire setup. Clamp the extrusion, use a straight guide, and support the full length on both sides of the cut. Keep the base plate flat and move the saw along the guide without twisting. A sideways correction changes the tooth engagement and can make a clean feed look like a blade problem.

**Band saws:** Feed is primarily downforce rather than hand speed. The goal is enough pressure to keep the teeth cutting without bending the blade or crushing the profile. On a horizontal band saw, a controlled hydraulic downfeed is valuable because it prevents the inconsistent pressure common with hand-fed cuts.

**Hacksaws:** A consistent full stroke is more important than frantic speed. Apply pressure during the cutting stroke, release it on the return, and keep the blade aligned. Short, rapid strokes often alternate between rubbing and sudden tooth overload. A hacksaw remains practical for a few small cuts, but the operator must accept more filing and deburring.

**Abrasive chop saws:** Feed technique cannot make a steel abrasive wheel suitable for aluminum. The wheel can load with soft metal, generate excessive heat, and leave a poor edge. If the machine is used for aluminum, it needs a proper nonferrous-metal blade and adequate workholding; otherwise, a different cutting method is safer and more predictable.

**CNC and automatic saws:** Automated equipment does not eliminate feed physics; it makes feed repeatable. The programmed feed must account for blade geometry, profile support, alloy, and cut length. For repeated production, that consistency can matter more than maximum cutting speed.

## A practical method for tuning aluminum cutting feed rate

1. **Confirm the blade before changing technique.** Use a sharp blade designed for nonferrous metal. On a circular or miter saw, a carbide TCG blade with a neutral or negative hook is often a sound starting point. On a band saw, choose tooth pitch and blade construction for the wall thickness. Feed adjustments cannot compensate for an unsuitable or damaged blade.

2. **Make the workholding rigid.** Clean chips from the fence and table, clamp the extrusion near the cut, and support both the retained stock and the offcut. If the workpiece moves, the cut may show burrs or chatter that look like feed problems.

3. **Use a matching scrap piece.** Test on the same alloy, profile, and orientation used in the finished work. A solid test bar does not accurately predict behavior on a hollow T-slot extrusion.

4. **Start with a steady, moderate feed.** Do not begin by barely moving the blade. Let the teeth engage continuously while keeping the saw aligned. The first test should produce recognizable chips, a stable motor sound, and no visible blade deflection.

5. **Change one variable at a time.** If the edge is smeared and the teeth are loading, clean the blade and increase feed slightly or reduce RPM if the equipment allows it. If the motor bogs or the profile deforms, reduce feed pressure and inspect support. Changing blade, RPM, lubricant, and feed simultaneously makes the result impossible to diagnose.

6. **Inspect the exit side.** The blade may enter cleanly and tear the final wall as it exits. Support the cutoff, maintain pressure through the last section, and avoid stopping with the blade buried in the profile.

7. **Record a repeatable setting.** For batch work, note the blade type, tooth count or pitch, RPM, profile, feed technique, and result. Blade condition changes over time, so inspect it after a run rather than assuming the original setting remains valid.

Aluminum chips are sharp and can be hot. Keep the guard in place, wear eye protection, clamp the stock, and stop and isolate power before clearing chips or inspecting the blade.

## Reading the cut as a diagnostic report

**Aluminum welded to the teeth:** The blade may be underfed, too hot, dull, or the wrong geometry. Clean the blade using the manufacturer’s approved method, verify chip clearance, and retest with a steady feed. Do not simply apply more force to a contaminated blade.

**A heavy burr at the exit:** The offcut may be unsupported, the feed may be too aggressive, or the blade may have too few or damaged teeth. Improve support first, then reduce feed pressure only enough to prevent tooth overload.

**An angled cut:** Excessive feed can deflect the blade, but a loose fence, poor clamping, or unsupported stock can do the same. Correct the mechanical setup before trying to steer the saw through the material.

**Chatter marks:** Intermittent tooth engagement usually points to vibration, inadequate support, a loose blade, or inconsistent feed. Pushing harder often makes chatter worse. Secure the profile and maintain continuous engagement.

**A crushed thin wall:** Feed pressure is too high for the section, or the cutting action is too aggressive. Reduce downforce, add appropriate internal or external support, and consider a band saw.

**The first cuts are clean and later cuts deteriorate:** Heat and blade buildup are accumulating. Inspect the blade after stopping the machine, clear the gullets, allow the equipment to cool if necessary, and review whether the feed has become more hesitant as resistance increased.

## Choosing a tool means choosing a feed-control system

For a frame project requiring repeated square cuts on standard T-slot extrusion, a miter saw is effective because its fence and pivot make a consistent feed easy. The advantage is not simply that the blade spins quickly; it is that the operator can reproduce the same descent on every piece.

For long stock, a circular saw becomes practical when a guide rail, clamps, and full-length supports keep the forward feed straight. Without those controls, portability comes at the cost of inconsistent chip load and squareness.

For thin-wall tubing or profiles that deform under impact, a band saw earns its place because it provides a gentler and more controllable feed. Its slower cutting speed is often a benefit when dimensional preservation matters more than cycle time.

For dozens or hundreds of identical pieces, professional automatic or CNC cutting can be the most economical choice because it holds the feed, alignment, and repeatability constant. When requesting a quote, specify cut-length tolerance, squareness, acceptable burr size, alloy, profile, and whether secondary deburring is required.

The deciding question is simple: can the selected method maintain a continuous chip-making feed without bending the blade, crushing the profile, or overheating the cutting edge? If the answer is no, changing tools is more productive than trying to perfect hand pressure on an unsuitable setup.

> The right feed is not the slowest feed. It is the steadiest pressure that creates a real chip while keeping the blade cool, the profile supported, and the cut square.
