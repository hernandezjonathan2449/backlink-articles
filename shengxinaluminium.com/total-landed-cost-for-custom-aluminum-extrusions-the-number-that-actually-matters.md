# Total Landed Cost for Custom Aluminum Extrusions: The Number That Actually Matters

## Total landed cost beats unit price every time

A quote for custom aluminum extrusions can look clean and competitive until the missing pieces show up. Freight, tooling, packaging, customs brokerage, scrap, and lead time often sit outside the headline number. That is why a quote that looks cheaper on paper can end up costing more in the warehouse.

The problem is not that suppliers always mislead buyers. The problem is that many quotes are built on different assumptions, and those assumptions are rarely visible unless someone asks the right questions. A disciplined read of [custom extrusion pricing](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/how-to-price-custom-aluminum-extrusions-without-getting-burned_n844) starts by stripping away the headline and asking one question: what does each usable part really cost once it arrives, passes inspection, and is ready to ship again?

## The trap hidden inside a low quote

Ex-works price is only a starting point. It tells you what leaves the plant, not what lands in your inventory. A buyer comparing two suppliers by piece price alone is comparing half the story.

That matters because custom aluminum extrusion projects usually carry several layers of cost that do not appear in the per-piece number:

- tooling or die cost
- sample approval and first article work
- packing for export or domestic transit
- freight and port handling
- customs duties, tariffs, and brokerage
- scrap allowance and process yield
- downstream machining or finishing
- inventory carry while material is in transit

The lowest quote often wins attention because it is easy to read. But if it excludes even one of these items, it is not a true comparison. A quote can be lower by 10 percent and still end up more expensive after the shipment clears customs or the finishing line rejects a batch.

## Compare quotes as delivered cost per usable part

The most useful pricing lens is not cost per piece shipped. It is cost per usable part delivered.

That phrase matters because a part that arrives damaged, out of tolerance, or unfinished is not a usable part. Neither is a part sitting in a bonded warehouse waiting for paperwork, nor a cut length that needs rework before it can enter assembly.

A proper landed-cost view includes:

1. **Base extrusion price** - the quote for the profile itself.
2. **Tooling amortization** - the die cost spread across the order quantity.
3. **Freight and handling** - air, ocean, truck, fuel surcharges, terminal fees, and last-mile delivery.
4. **Trade costs** - duties, tariffs, brokerage, and compliance paperwork.
5. **Processing losses** - scrap, rejects, overrun, and startup waste.
6. **Working capital cost** - the money tied up while the parts move, clear, and sit in inventory.

This is where [landed cost math](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/how-to-price-custom-aluminum-extrusions-without-getting-burned_n844) changes the conversation. A quote is no longer a single number. It becomes a stack of numbers that either support the purchase or expose a weak supplier offer.

## A simple example that changes the answer

Two suppliers quote the same profile: a 6063-T5 extrusion with a modest cut-to-length requirement and a clear anodized finish. The order is 1,200 pieces.

### Supplier A
- Piece price: $7.95
- Tooling: $1,100
- Freight and local delivery: $280
- Included setup: yes

**Total:** $10,920

### Supplier B
- Piece price: $6.80
- Tooling: $850
- Ocean freight, port fees, and documents: $1,250
- Duty and brokerage: $620
- Inland receiving and transfer: $180
- Inventory carrying cost for seven weeks in transit: about $220
- Scrap and overrun reserve: $245

**Total:** $11,525

On the front page, Supplier B looks 14 percent cheaper. After the full landed-cost stack, Supplier A is actually about 5.5 percent less expensive.

That gap can widen further if the lower-priced supplier has a higher reject rate, longer transit time, or a finish quality problem that forces rework. The quote that looked like a bargain becomes the more expensive sourcing decision.

## Why freight and trade costs distort the headline number

Freight is the easiest hidden cost to underestimate because it often arrives as a separate invoice long after the quote is approved. For overseas sourcing, that invoice can include more than transport:

- ocean or air freight
- export documentation
- port handling
- customs clearance
- duties and tariffs
- domestic drayage after port arrival

Those charges can easily erase the difference between two quotes that were only a dollar apart on piece price. The same applies to domestic freight when the supplier is far from the final assembly site or when the order ships in multiple small drops instead of one consolidated run.

Trade costs are especially dangerous because they are not always fixed. A quote that looks acceptable today can become unattractive if tariff treatment changes, the commodity surcharge moves, or the customs classification is challenged.

## Why scrap rate belongs in the quote review

Scrap is one of the most overlooked factors in custom aluminum extrusion pricing. A supplier that assumes a 2 percent reject rate and another that assumes 8 percent are not quoting the same job, even if every other line looks identical.

That difference matters because scrap affects two things at once:

- the amount of aluminum purchased
- the number of finished parts that actually ship

If a profile has tight tolerances, thin walls, or a demanding finish, yield can move the final price in a meaningful way. A buyer who ignores scrap is usually comparing theoretical parts, not production-ready parts.

The same logic applies to overruns. Some extrusions need extra material to compensate for startup losses, process variation, or packaging breakage. If the supplier prices only exact-net quantity, the buyer may face a second order later at a much worse effective price.

## Tooling cost only matters when it is spread correctly

Die cost gets attention because it is visible, but visibility does not make it simple. A $900 die and a $1,500 die can be either cheap or expensive depending on the order size and expected annual volume.

The useful calculation is not "What does the die cost?" It is "What does the die cost per usable part over the full program life?"

That is why a lower tooling quote can be misleading. If a supplier offers a cheap die but the profile must be replaced sooner, or if the lower price comes with a short production guarantee, the apparent savings evaporate. The die should be evaluated alongside projected volume, expected reorders, and any promised tooling refunds.

## Inventory carry has a real price tag

Lead time is a cost even when nobody invoices it directly. A six-week ocean transit means money is tied up longer, more safety stock is needed, and production planning has less flexibility. That creates a carrying cost that should be part of the sourcing decision.

For many buyers, carrying cost is small on a single order and substantial across a year. The math grows quickly when the buyer must:

- hold extra stock to avoid line stoppages
- pay for warehouse space
- finance inventory before it is consumed
- absorb the risk of slow-moving material

A quote that appears cheaper because it leaves out inventory carry is not cheaper. It simply pushes the expense into another line of the business.

## The questions that reveal the real number

A good supplier quote can survive scrutiny. A weak one starts falling apart as soon as the assumptions are tested.

The fastest way to separate the two is to ask for each of these in writing:

- Is the price ex-works, FOB, CIF, or delivered?
- What is included in tooling, samples, and first article approval?
- What freight mode and transit time were used in the quote?
- Are duties, tariffs, brokerage, and port fees included?
- What scrap rate or yield assumption was used?
- What finish specification is priced, and what is excluded?
- Are rework, rejects, and overrun handling covered?
- What payment terms affect cash flow or financing cost?

If a supplier cannot answer those questions clearly, the quote is not ready for comparison. It is only a starting estimate.

## The practical rule that keeps buyers out of trouble

> Compare custom aluminum extrusion quotes only after converting every line item into landed cost per usable part.

That rule protects against the most common sourcing mistake: choosing the lowest sticker price instead of the lowest true cost.

When two quotes are reduced to the same basis, the better choice often becomes obvious. Sometimes the higher quoted price really is the better deal because it includes freight, lower scrap, faster turnaround, or fewer quality risks. Sometimes the cheapest quote remains cheapest after all the math is done. The point is not to favor expensive suppliers. The point is to stop buying from incomplete numbers.

A buyer who prices on landed cost asks better questions, sees hidden assumptions sooner, and negotiates from a position of clarity. That is the difference between a purchase that looks good in procurement and a purchase that actually performs on the factory floor.
