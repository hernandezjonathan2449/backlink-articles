# Facade Extrusion Design: How Custom Aluminum Profiles Control Curtain Wall Performance

## The real job of a facade extrusion

A curtain wall does not fail because aluminum is weak. It fails when the extrusion geometry gives water, heat, and movement a path that was never intended. That is why [custom facade extrusions](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/custom-aluminum-extrusions-for-facades-from-concept-to-curtain-wall_n821) matter so much: the profile is not a decorative trim piece, it is the operating logic of the wall.

In field reviews, the most expensive problems usually trace back to one assumption: treating the section as a shape instead of a system. A mullion may look clean on the drawing, but if it does not control expansion, hold the gasket in the right compression range, and move water toward a drain path, the beautiful section becomes a liability.

## Movement is the hidden load most drawings underplay

Aluminum moves more than steel, and facades feel that movement every day. A 3-meter mullion can change length by nearly 3 mm across a 40°C temperature swing. A 6-meter member can move close to 7 mm. That is not a theoretical number buried in a textbook; that is enough to change seal compression, stress corner joints, and turn a tight perimeter joint into a leak path.

The mistake is to treat thermal movement as something the anchor handles. The anchor only locates the unit. The extrusion has to absorb the change without pushing the glass, distorting the sightline, or loading the sealant line until it cracks. On tall buildings, the stack effect makes the problem more visible because every floor line adds its own tolerance and deflection.

That is why the best facade sections create deliberate places for movement:

- slotted fixing points instead of hard locks where the member needs to slide
- gasket chambers that stay effective even when the section shifts a few millimeters
- cover caps and pressure plates that do not become the structural path
- joints that separate structural restraint from weather sealing

If those functions are merged casually, the wall may look fine on day one and still fail in the first hot summer or cold snap.

## Thermal breaks only work when the section supports them

The thermal break is often sold as the answer, but it only works when the extrusion was designed around it. A polyamide strip inside a poorly balanced profile does not solve thermal bridging if the metal still creates short paths through fasteners, plates, or accessory clips. Heat always takes the easiest route.

That shows up in two familiar ways. First, condensation on the interior leg of the frame when humid indoor air meets a cold aluminum surface. Second, energy performance that looks acceptable in a brochure but falls apart at the slab edge, corner conditions, or perimeter mullion. The geometry around the break matters just as much as the break itself.

A strong section usually does three things well:

1. separates exterior and interior load paths as much as possible
2. gives the thermal break enough depth to work without overstressing the legs
3. keeps the fasteners and accessory hardware from bypassing the break

In other words, the section has to make thermal resistance a structural decision, not an afterthought. For projects where the facade is expected to meet aggressive energy targets, that is often the difference between a code-compliant design and a wall that requires field fixes.

## Water management is a geometry problem, not a sealant problem

Most leaks do not start with a missing bead of sealant. They start with a profile that never gave water a clean route out. Rain does not need much encouragement. If a pocket becomes a shelf, if a return leg is too shallow, or if capillary action can bridge a tiny gap, water will travel where the design invited it.

A well-formed extrusion handles water in layers:

- it prevents direct driving rain from reaching the interior line
- it equalizes pressure so water is not forced inward by suction
- it drains collected water toward a controlled exit
- it uses capillary breaks so film water cannot creep back inside

That is why drainage slots, internal chambers, and drip edges matter more than they appear to in section view. A 1 mm error in the location of a weep path may not sound serious until it is repeated across 200 linear meters of facade and every joint starts holding water after a storm.

Sealant still matters, but it is a secondary defense. If the extrusion depends on sealant alone, it is already over the line into maintenance-driven performance. Once UV, movement, and installer variation enter the picture, the margin disappears quickly.

## Tolerances are performance data

A common mistake is to list tolerances as if they belong to manufacturing only. On a facade, tolerances are part of performance. A gasket groove that is off by a fraction of a millimeter can change compression enough to affect air and water tightness. A profile with too much bow can force installers to preload the frame and transfer unintended stress into the glass edge.

This becomes more serious on long members. A six-meter mullion with visible twist is not simply unattractive; it changes how pressure plates seat, how cover caps snap on, and how adjacent units align at the transom joint. The result is a chain reaction of small misfits that eventually shows up as rattling, leakage, or a broken finish at installation points.

The better question is not whether a profile can be extruded. It is whether the critical dimensions can be held where the wall actually needs them:

- glazing pocket depth
- gasket compression zone
- thermal break slot width
- anchor engagement surfaces
- drainage and weep features
- straightness across installed lengths

That is also where a disciplined [custom extrusion workflow](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/custom-aluminum-extrusions-for-facades-from-concept-to-curtain-wall_n821) matters. When geometry, fabrication, and installation are coordinated early, the extrusion can be optimized for the real tolerances on site instead of idealized numbers on paper.

## The best profile reduces the number of decisions on site

The strongest custom sections do more than solve one problem. They combine functions so the installer has fewer opportunities to improvise. A single extrusion can carry the structural flange, the drainage route, the thermal break pocket, and the fixing leg if the section is developed with that purpose from the start.

That consolidation matters because every extra part adds another interface:

- another fastener line
- another place for water to sit
- another tolerance stack-up
- another surface that can misalign or fatigue

The tradeoff is that integration must be designed carefully. Overloading the profile with too many features can create thin webs, awkward metal flow in the die, or brittle details that are hard to finish and hard to assemble. The point is not to make one profile do everything. The point is to make each function deliberate, visible, and testable.

When that happens, the extrusion stops being a commodity piece of aluminum and becomes an engineered interface between structure, weather, and appearance. That is the real reason custom profiles matter on facades: they are where the building decides whether it can move, stay dry, and hold its line for decades.

## What to check before a profile gets approved

A useful review never starts with color or finish. It starts with failure paths. Before a section is frozen, it should answer a few hard questions:

- Where does the frame move, and where is it locked?
- What stops water from crossing the weather line?
- If the thermal break is doing its job, what metal path still remains?
- Which dimensions are critical to glass fit and gasket compression?
- What happens if installation is 2 mm off at the field joint?

If those answers are vague, the drawing is not ready. The fastest way to fix a facade in the field is still to design it so the field has nothing to fix.

A custom extrusion succeeds when it turns invisible after installation. No rattles, no cold spots, no trapped water, no distorted joints. The section has done its job when the building looks simple, because the complexity was resolved inside the profile long before the first unit went up.
