# Aluminum Extrusion Cost: Compare Parts, Not Quotes

## Aluminum Extrusion Cost Is a Part-Cost Problem

The most expensive quoting mistake in extrusion sourcing is treating the lowest price per kilogram as the lowest-cost option. A supplier can offer $3.65/kg and still produce a more expensive project than a supplier quoting $4.20/kg once tooling, yield, finishing, freight, and rejected parts are included.

The useful procurement question is not, What is the quoted price? It is: **What will it cost to receive one conforming part at the required length, finish, quantity, and destination?**

An extrusion quote is a conditional cost model. Its result depends on the assumptions behind the number. The same profile may carry a very different effective price when ordered as 500 cut pieces instead of 50,000 pieces because die amortization, setup, scrap, inspection, and shipping are spread across a different volume.

## Why a $/kg Quote Can Mislead

Aluminum extrusion suppliers commonly quote on different commercial bases:

- Raw or finished aluminum per kilogram
- Profile per foot or meter
- Cut piece pricing
- Ex-works shipment value
- Delivered pricing that includes freight and duties
- A complete part price that includes finishing and machining

Those figures are not interchangeable. A low $/kg quote may exclude the operations that make the profile usable, while a higher quote may already include them.

The omitted items often include:

- Alloy and temper surcharges
- Die design, machining, testing, and correction
- Press setup and extrusion conversion
- Stretching, straightening, and cut-to-length work
- Anodizing, powder coating, or PVDF finishing
- Drilling, tapping, CNC machining, or other secondary operations
- Packaging designed to prevent scratches or deformation
- Freight, customs, tariffs, and local delivery
- Inspection, certification, and replacement of rejected pieces
- Scrap caused by startup, end cuts, saw kerf, or low first-pass yield

An omission does not automatically make a quote poor. It makes the quote incomplete for comparison. Two suppliers can be commercially honest while still presenting numbers that appear to disagree because each has included a different portion of the work.

## Normalize Every Quote to the Same Accepted Part

The only reliable comparison is to place every supplier on the same physical and commercial scope. A detailed [extrusion cost breakdown](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-cost-breakdown-a-few-factors-that-make-or-break-your-quote_n1028) helps identify the usual cost categories, but the decisive step is converting those categories into a common delivered-part basis.

Lock down these variables before ranking suppliers.

1. **Define the part precisely.** Specify the drawing revision, alloy, temper, net weight, finished length, length tolerance, straightness, surface requirements, and critical dimensions. A supplier quoting a standard 6-meter length is not directly comparable with one quoting inspected 1,000 mm pieces.

2. **State the required quantity as accepted pieces.** Ten thousand shipped pieces are not necessarily ten thousand usable pieces. The RFQ should state the number of conforming parts required and whether the supplier must replace rejected material at its own expense.

3. **Separate first release from program volume.** A die used for 500 pieces has a dramatically different cost per part than the same die used over 50,000 pieces. Annual demand and the number of planned releases affect the right allocation.

4. **List every required operation.** Extrusion, stretching, cutting, drilling, tapping, machining, heat treatment, finishing, inspection, and packaging should appear separately or be clearly identified as included.

5. **Specify the finish by performance, not just color.** Anodizing thickness, powder-coat system, gloss, color standard, appearance class, and masking requirements can change both cost and yield.

6. **Fix the delivery basis.** Ex-works, FOB, CIF, DAP, and DDP prices shift freight, customs, tax, and risk between buyer and supplier. The destination should be the same for every quote.

7. **Define metal-price adjustments.** If the aluminum component follows an index, identify the base date, regional premium, currency, adjustment frequency, and whether the conversion charge remains fixed.

The underlying calculation is straightforward:

> Cost per accepted part = (material + extrusion conversion + tooling allocation + finishing + secondary operations + packaging + freight and duties + inspection + expected quality cost − credits) ÷ accepted quantity

Material usage also needs a yield adjustment:

> Required chargeable material = net part weight × accepted quantity ÷ usable yield

If a profile weighs 1.20 kg and the order requires 10,000 accepted pieces, the net aluminum requirement is 12,000 kg. At a 92% usable yield, the process may require approximately 13,043 kg of chargeable material. Ignoring that difference understates the project cost by more than 1,000 kg before finishing or shipping is considered.

## A Quote Comparison That Reverses the Ranking

Consider a first order for 10,000 accepted, 1,000 mm anodized profiles. Each conforming profile weighs 1.20 kg. The following figures are illustrative; their purpose is to show how quote ranking changes when scope is normalized.

### Supplier A: Lower headline rate

Supplier A quotes $3.65/kg on an ex-works basis. The die, anodizing, cutting, packaging, freight, and quality allowance are separate.

- Usable yield: 92%
- Chargeable material: 12,000 ÷ 0.92 = 13,043 kg
- Profile charge: 13,043 × $3.65 = approximately $47,607
- Anodizing: 13,043 × $0.28 = approximately $3,652
- Cutting and packaging: 10,000 × $0.18 = $1,800
- New die: $2,000
- Freight and duties: $5,200
- Expected quality and expedite allowance: $2,000

The estimated project total is approximately **$62,259**, or **$6.23 per accepted part**.

### Supplier B: Higher headline rate

Supplier B quotes $4.20/kg on a delivered bundle that includes anodizing, cutting, packaging, freight, and duties. The die is separate.

- Usable yield: 98%
- Chargeable material: 12,000 ÷ 0.98 = 12,245 kg
- Delivered bundle: 12,245 × $4.20 = approximately $51,429
- New die: $1,400
- Expected quality allowance: $500

The estimated project total is approximately **$53,329**, or **$5.33 per accepted part**.

Supplier B appears to be about 15% more expensive when judged only by the kilogram rate. After normalizing the scope, Supplier B is approximately $8,930 less expensive for the order, reducing the effective cost per accepted part by about 14%.

The difference is not a negotiating trick. Supplier A's lower number describes less of the finished supply obligation. Its lower yield also increases the amount of aluminum and finishing work required. The relevant comparison is the total cash needed to receive conforming parts, not the most attractive number on the first line of the quotation.

## Yield Is the Quiet Multiplier in Aluminum Extrusion Cost

Yield is often treated as a manufacturing detail, but it directly changes material cost, finishing cost, delivery weight, and the number of saleable pieces. Ask suppliers to distinguish among three different measures:

- **Material yield:** the percentage of input aluminum that becomes usable profile
- **First-pass yield:** the percentage that meets requirements without rework
- **Shipment acceptance:** the percentage of delivered pieces accepted by the buyer

A supplier may quote net weight while recovering startup scrap through a setup fee or minimum order. Another may quote on chargeable weight and build the scrap allowance directly into the per-kilogram price. Both approaches can work, but they must be visible.

Cut planning creates another source of hidden loss. Suppose a nominal 6,000 mm extrusion must produce 1,000 mm pieces and the saw removes 3 mm per cut. Six pieces already require at least 6,015 mm when five internal kerfs are included, before end trim or damaged sections are considered. A supplier running exactly 6,000 mm lengths cannot achieve the intuitive six-pieces-per-length ratio without changing the run length or accepting additional scrap.

Finishing can also make weight-based comparisons unreliable. A thin-walled profile may have a large surface area relative to its mass. If anodizing or powder coating is priced by surface area, the finishing cost per kilogram may be much higher than for a heavy solid profile, even when both carry the same aluminum price.

A credible quote should state whether yield assumptions apply to input weight, shipped weight, or accepted output. If the answer is unclear, use a conservative allowance rather than assuming that every kilogram purchased becomes a usable part.

## Tooling Must Be Viewed as Both Cash Outlay and Unit Cost

A die is paid early, but its economic effect depends on how many accepted parts use it. A $2,000 die allocated across different order sizes produces very different results:

- 500 accepted pieces: $4.00 per part
- 10,000 accepted pieces: $0.20 per part
- 50,000 accepted pieces: $0.04 per part

That is why the first-order cost and the recurring cost should be shown separately. A supplier that offers a lower die charge may recover the difference through a higher profile price, while a supplier offering free tooling may require a minimum annual volume, exclusive purchasing, or a commitment to use the die only at its facility.

The phrase free tooling should trigger a scope check. Confirm:

- Who owns the physical die
- Whether the buyer can receive or transfer it
- How long the supplier will store it
- Who pays for die correction after the first trial
- Whether normal wear and replacement are included
- What happens if the drawing changes
- Whether the quoted die supports the required production volume

For a low-volume order, tooling can dominate the economics. For a long-running program, press efficiency, yield, finishing, and metal-price terms usually matter more. The same quote cannot be judged correctly without knowing which volume scenario applies.

## Separate Metal Volatility from Manufacturing Value

Aluminum pricing often includes a metal component tied to an index or regional premium and a conversion component covering labor, energy, press time, tooling recovery, and overhead. These components should not move by the same percentage automatically.

For example, assume a normalized part cost is $100 and 55% of that amount is metal-linked. If the applicable aluminum index rises by 8% while all other charges remain fixed, the direct increase is approximately $4.40 per part, not $8.00. The actual adjustment may differ because of premiums, currency, or supplier contract terms, but the calculation shows why the pricing formula matters.

A buyer should request:

- The metal-price basis and reference date
- The regional premium or billet surcharge
- The portion of the quote that remains fixed
- The adjustment trigger and timing
- Any price floor or ceiling
- The quote expiration date
- The currency used for each component

A fixed price may be valuable when the production schedule is certain and the supplier has priced the risk correctly. An indexed price may be safer for a long program with volatile aluminum markets. Neither is automatically cheaper; the correct choice depends on who carries the price risk and whether that risk is priced transparently.

## Quality Risk Belongs in the Comparison

A quote is not low-cost if it produces late or unusable parts. Quality risk should be treated as an expected financial cost rather than an abstract supplier concern.

Assume an order requires 10,000 parts. A 3% rejection rate creates 300 affected pieces. If each rejected piece costs an additional $18 in replacement freight, inspection, handling, and administrative work, the expected direct impact is $5,400 before production downtime. At a 0.5% rejection rate, the same allowance would be $900. The $4,500 difference can outweigh a modest gap in quoted piece price.

The calculation should use actual supplier history where available. Ask for first-pass yield, defect categories, corrective-action procedures, sample approval requirements, and replacement lead time. A supplier may offer free replacement material, but that does not eliminate the cost of missed assembly slots or emergency transportation.

Schedule risk can also be modeled. If a $50,000 order requires six extra weeks of safety stock and the carrying cost is 12% annually, the financing and storage effect is roughly $692 before warehouse space, insurance, or the commercial cost of delay. A launch-critical component may justify a higher unit price from a supplier with stable lead times because the risk-adjusted cost is lower.

## Write the RFQ Around the Finished Obligation

The strongest RFQs make the desired comparison unavoidable. A useful request can state:

> Quote 10,000 accepted pieces of the attached 6063-T5 profile, finished at 1,000 mm, with the specified anodizing and inspection requirements. Show first-order and repeat-order pricing separately. List die cost, chargeable weight, yield assumption, finishing, cutting, packaging, freight, duties, lead time, replacement terms, and any metal-index adjustment as separate line items.

The response should also identify:

- Price per kilogram and effective price per accepted piece
- Quantity used to amortize the die
- Tool ownership and correction policy
- Included inspection and documentation
- Packaging configuration and shipment quantity
- Incoterm and named delivery location
- Quote validity and escalation formula
- Minimum order or release quantity
- Conditions that would change the quoted price

Requesting both the first-order total and the repeat-order price prevents a common error: choosing a supplier based on an attractive recurring rate while overlooking a first-release die and setup cost that makes the initial project uncompetitive.

## The Metric That Should Make the Decision

For low-volume projects, tooling and setup may dominate. For high-volume programs, yield, conversion efficiency, and metal-price terms generally carry more weight. For thin, highly finished profiles, surface area and finishing yield can matter more than the aluminum rate. For overseas supply, freight, duties, inventory, and quality risk can reverse an apparent factory-price advantage.

Those differences do not change the central rule. **The winning aluminum extrusion quote is the one with the lowest risk-adjusted delivered cost per accepted part under the same assumptions.**

When suppliers provide different scopes, the lowest number is not proof of savings. It is a signal that some portion of the finished supply obligation has not yet been priced. Normalize the obligation first, then negotiate the line items that genuinely create value.
