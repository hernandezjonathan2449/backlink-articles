# Aluminum Window Frame Extrusions: The System Is the Specification

## The Profile Is Not the Product

A die produces a continuous metal section; it does not produce an airtight, watertight, load-bearing window. That distinction explains a large share of procurement failures. A profile can meet its alloy, dimensional, and coating requirements while the assembled window still leaks at a corner, binds at a roller, transmits heat through a fastener, or bows far enough to unload its gasket.

Finished behavior emerges from a chain: billet chemistry, section geometry, thermal break, machining, joints, gaskets, glass, hardware, and installation. A weakness at any interface can override a strong result earlier in the chain.

A catalog line such as 6063-T5, 70 series, 1.8 mm wall, and 1.2 kg/m is useful raw-component information. An [extrusion specification guide](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminium-window-frame-extrusions-decode-specs-dodge-red-flags_n1041) can help decode alloy, temper, wall thickness, series depth, and finish claims. The harder question comes afterward: will this exact section, joined with these exact parts, deliver the required air, water, structural, and thermal performance at the project's opening size?

That question changes buying behavior. Instead of treating the extrusion as the finished product, treat it as one link in a tested system.

## Why Isolated Numbers Mislead

### Alloy and Temper Are Not a Stiffness Calculation

6063-T5 is common in architectural windows because it pushes cleanly through complex dies and takes anodizing and paint well. 6061-T6 can offer much higher strength and is useful when heavy mullions or reinforcements carry demanding loads. Typical reference values put 6061-T6 near 310 MPa tensile strength and 6063-T5 near 190 MPa, although the applicable standard and product form control the actual minimum.

Those values describe material resistance to yielding or fracture. They do not directly predict how much a mullion deflects. Most 6000-series aluminum alloys have a similar elastic modulus, roughly 69 GPa. Initial elastic deflection therefore depends heavily on span, support conditions, and the section's moment of inertia. Changing from 6063 to 6061 may improve the margin against permanent deformation, but it does not magically make a shallow section rigid.

That distinction matters for slim sightlines. A narrow-looking frame may need more depth, a stronger internal shape, or a reinforcement rather than simply a stronger alloy.

### Series Depth Is Not Wall Thickness

A 70 series profile generally refers to an approximately 70 mm construction depth. It does not mean the walls are 70 mm thick, and it does not tell you whether the section is suitable for a particular span. Wall thickness affects screw retention, local durability, and material consumption; depth and shape often have a larger effect on bending stiffness.

One calculated comparison illustrates the point: a 40 × 80 mm hollow section with 1.8 mm walls can have roughly 83 percent more bending inertia than a 40 × 60 mm section with 2.0 mm walls, depending on the actual geometry. The deeper section can be stiffer even though its nominal wall is thinner. A kilogram-per-meter figure helps estimate material cost, but it is not a substitute for a structural calculation.

### The Same Profile Can Serve Different Windows

A 6063-T5 sash might work well in a small residential casement and be inadequate in a tall sliding door. The alloy did not change; the span, glass weight, operating forces, wind pressure, and support conditions did. A profile listing that omits those boundary conditions cannot establish suitability.

## Interfaces Are Where a Profile Becomes a Window

A window is a collection of mating dimensions. The extrusion is successful only when those dimensions remain compatible after cutting, machining, finishing, joining, glazing, and installation.

### Tolerance Stack-Up Changes Seal Compression

Extrusion tolerances may be around ±0.1 to ±0.3 mm, depending on the feature, size, alloy, and governing standard. That number applies to a feature on the extruded length; it is not the tolerance of the assembled window.

Suppose a frame and sash each have a critical feature at a worst-case ±0.15 mm position. Their relative gap can shift by 0.30 mm before saw accuracy, corner squareness, crimping, hardware adjustment, gasket variation, and thermal-break assembly enter the calculation. A shift that looks minor on a drawing can move a gasket from its intended compression range.

Too little compression allows air and water through. Too much compression raises operating force, wears hardware, and can make a sash appear warped even when the metal is straight. The same issue appears in glazing pockets: an incorrect groove or bead position changes glass bite, gasket seating, and the clearance needed for building movement.

### Hardware and Glass Expose Hidden Mismatches

Rollers, hinges, locks, and handles rely on exact channel dimensions and screw engagement. A channel that is slightly narrow can bind a carrier; one that is too large can leave a fastener with inadequate holding power. Hardware from a similar-looking system is not automatically compatible.

Glass creates another constraint. A profile drawing must accommodate the actual insulated glass unit, edge clearance, setting blocks, bead, gasket, and drainage path. A 44 mm glass unit may fit the pocket on paper but leave too little bite for the bead or too little room for thermal movement. A fabricator who substitutes a generic gasket can change the effective glazing height enough to create a failure that no alloy certificate will reveal.

### Corners and Drainage Decide Water Performance

Water resistance depends on more than the thickness of the outer wall. A fabricated frame needs accurate miters, sealed or mechanically joined corners, functioning weep paths, sill slope, end dams, and pressure management within the cavities.

In first-article inspections, recurring problems include a corner that is not fully crimped, a drainage slot blocked by a sealant bead, and a gasket that stops short at the joint. Each defect is small; together they turn a compliant extrusion into a leaking window. The profile may be dimensionally perfect while the fabricated assembly is not.

## Thermal Performance Belongs to the Complete Assembly

Aluminum's high conductivity makes the system boundary especially important. A solid metal path can carry exterior cold to the room-side surface, where condensation forms if the surface temperature falls below the indoor dew point. The glass may be highly insulating while the frame edge remains cold.

A thermal break interrupts that path with a low-conductivity material. Common approaches include a glass-fiber-reinforced polyamide strip crimped into prepared channels and a poured polyurethane barrier whose bridging metal is removed after curing. Both can work, but the material name alone does not prove performance.

The strip must match the cavity width, seat fully in the knurled channels, and retain its position under temperature cycling and structural load. A poured system must be filled, cured, and debridged to the intended depth. Screws, corner keys, reinforcements, glazing beads, and hardware can create secondary thermal bridges even when the main frame has a thermal break.

Thermal terminology also creates avoidable confusion:

- **Uf** describes the frame or frame-and-sash portion under a defined calculation method.
- **Ug** describes the center-of-glass value.
- **Uw** describes the complete window, including frame, glass, edge spacer, and specified size.

A profile supplier that publishes one U-value without identifying which value it represents, the window size, the glass, the spacer, and the boundary conditions has not given a comparable performance claim. The same extrusion can produce different Uw values when the glass area, spacer, gasket, thermal-break width, or sash ratio changes.

For a cold-climate project, the practical test is not whether the listing says thermally broken. It is whether the exact assembled window keeps its interior surfaces above the project's condensation risk while meeting the required whole-window U-factor.

## Air, Water, and Wind Ratings Belong to a Tested Configuration

North American performance ratings make the distinction explicit. ASTM E283 measures air leakage, ASTM E331 checks water penetration, and ASTM E330 evaluates structural performance under uniform pressure. Those tests are performed on a complete specimen with its frame, sash, glazing, gaskets, hardware, corners, and operating configuration.

A profile cannot pass E331 by itself. It has no sash meeting rail, no drainage system, and no locked gasket line until it is fabricated.

Design pressure also changes with the project. A ground-floor window in a sheltered neighborhood and a 20th-floor opening facing the ocean should not share a structural assumption merely because both use a 70 series frame. For perspective, a 3 m by 2.4 m opening exposed to 40 psf has roughly 3,100 pounds of total uniform pressure over its area before load sharing and support details are considered. The mullion, sash, hardware, anchors, and surrounding wall each participate in resisting that load.

That is why a small-window test report cannot automatically validate a much larger slider. The glass size, aspect ratio, number of locking points, mullion span, drainage pressure, and deflection limits all change. A credible report identifies the tested system and size, not just the alloy and profile family.

A supplier may cite NAFS, CE, ISO 9001, or an SGS report. Those references can be useful, but each has a defined scope. A quality-management certificate describes factory processes. A material standard describes the extrusion. A laboratory report describes the samples and configuration it tested. None of those documents turns an untested combination of profile, gasket, glass, and hardware into a rated window.

## Finish Quality Is Part of the Same Evidence Chain

Finish selection does not replace system engineering, but it reveals whether the specification is detailed enough to control real service conditions.

A coastal frame needs more than a color name or the phrase marine grade. Pretreatment, coating thickness, sealing of cut edges, compatible fasteners, drainage, and isolation of dissimilar metals all affect corrosion. An architectural anodizing specification may distinguish Class I at about 18 microns from Class II at about 10 microns under commonly used AAMA categories. A powder-coating specification should identify the coating class, pretreatment, adhesion, and weathering requirements rather than only a color reference.

The location of the finish matters too. A factory-coated six-meter extrusion is later cut, drilled, mitered, and crimped. Those operations expose edges and create contact points that the original coating test did not cover. If the fabrication method does not protect those areas, a high-grade coating cannot prevent corrosion starting at a cut end or fastener hole.

The evidence chain should therefore run in order:

1. The mill certificate verifies the alloy and temper for the relevant heat or lot.
2. Dimensional inspection verifies walls, grooves, pockets, straightness, and critical mating features.
3. Finish records verify pretreatment and film thickness.
4. First-article inspection verifies that machining, joining, gaskets, glass, and hardware fit together.
5. Complete-window testing verifies air, water, structural, and thermal behavior.

Each level answers a different question. None can stand in for the next.

## Specify the Window Outward, Not the Catalog Inward

The reliable procurement sequence starts with the finished opening and works back toward the extrusion.

### 1. Define the Boundary Conditions

Record the clear opening, supported spans, building height, exposure, design pressure, operating type, glass thickness, target whole-window U-factor, indoor humidity, and corrosion environment. A coastal high-rise, a bathroom casement, and a sheltered interior partition may all use aluminum, but their requirements are not interchangeable.

If the target is energy performance, specify Uw for the complete window rather than asking for an impressive-sounding profile U-value. If the target is wind resistance, state the design pressure and deflection limit rather than requesting a thicker wall by habit.

### 2. Create an Interface Control Drawing

Show the frame, sash, glazing bead, gasket grooves, thermal-break cavities, hardware channels, drainage slots, corner joints, and anchor zones in the same controlled drawing. Mark critical dimensions and tolerances individually. Give the drawing a revision number and identify which dimensions must remain compatible with the chosen hardware and glass.

This drawing is more valuable than a product photograph because it gives the extruder and fabricator the same definition of the part. It also makes substitutions visible. A supplier can propose a different alloy or wall thickness, but not silently change a gasket groove or hardware channel and still call the profile equivalent.

### 3. Request Evidence at the Right Level

Ask for the alloy and temper certificate, dimensional inspection data, finish specification, thermal-break material and retention method, and the complete-window test report. For a test report, compare the tested profile, glass, spacer, gaskets, hardware, corner method, operating type, and specimen size with the proposed unit.

A report that covers a fixed light does not automatically cover a slider. A report using a 24 mm insulated glass unit does not automatically cover a 44 mm unit. A test on a thermally broken version does not validate a non-thermally broken substitution.

### 4. Approve a Real First Article

A short sample of bare extrusion proves very little. The first article should be cut to production lengths, machined with the intended tools, joined using the production method, fitted with the actual thermal break, glazed with the specified unit, and operated with the specified hardware.

Check sash gaps, gasket compression, locking force, drainage, corner alignment, glass bite, coating damage, and thermal-break retention. For a high-rise, coastal, or unusually large opening, a project-specific mock-up or performance test is cheaper than discovering incompatibility after a container of profiles has been fabricated.

## What Plausible Quotes Can Hide

Consider two quotations that both say 6063-T5, 70 series, 1.8 mm wall, and powder coated.

The first includes a controlled gasket and hardware system, a defined thermal break, a tested corner method, and a report for a window close to the requested size. The second supplies a similar-looking section with no mating-component list and a test report for a different small window. The metal description is nearly identical; the project risk is not.

Now consider a quote that promotes 6061-T6 as automatically superior. If the section is shallow, the span is excessive, the thermal break is poorly retained, or the corner leaks, the higher material strength does not solve the actual failure mode. The stronger alloy may also be harder to extrude into intricate hollows and may offer a less uniform anodized appearance. Strength is valuable when the calculation needs it, not as a substitute for a compatible system.

The same logic applies to finishes. A premium fluoropolymer coating on a frame with blocked weeps is still a leaking frame. A well-anodized profile with incompatible fasteners can still develop galvanic corrosion. A heavier section with a poor gasket interface can still admit air.

## Red Flags That Reveal Profile-Only Selling

Treat these as requests for missing system evidence rather than automatic proof of bad faith:

- The listing gives alloy and color but no section drawing, critical tolerances, or mating-component information.
- A 50, 60, 70, or 80 series label is presented as proof of strength without a span, load, or deflection calculation.
- Wall thickness and kilograms per meter are used as the only comparison.
- A U-value appears without identifying Uf, Uw, glass, spacer, size, or calculation method.
- CE, ISO 9001, SGS, or RoHS badges are offered as proof of air, water, or structural performance.
- A test report names a different sash, glass thickness, gasket, hardware set, or specimen size.
- The supplier cannot state the thermal-break material, width, retention method, or inspection criteria.
- The quote calls a section custom but does not control drawing revisions, die ownership, or first-article approval.
- The supplier discourages a fit sample or treats a photograph as an adequate replacement for a dimensioned drawing.

The common issue behind these red flags is not that the extrusion is necessarily poor. It is that the evidence stops before the part becomes a window.

## The Decision Rule

Choose aluminum window frame extrusions by the performance chain they support, not by the most impressive isolated number. Alloy, temper, wall thickness, mass, series depth, coating, and thermal-break type are important inputs. The decisive evidence is whether those inputs remain compatible through machining, joining, glazing, hardware installation, and exposure to the actual site loads.

The question to put in front of a supplier is precise:

> Can this exact profile and assembly—using the specified glass, gaskets, hardware, thermal break, corner method, finish, and size—meet the required thermal, air, water, and structural performance?

A clear answer comes with controlled drawings, traceable certificates, first-article results, and a complete-system test. A vague answer comes with a catalog image, a color chart, and a row of badges. Only the first answer describes a window rather than a piece of aluminum.
