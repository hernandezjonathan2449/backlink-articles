# 6063 Aluminum Temper Selection: When to Choose T5, T6, or T4

## 6063 Aluminum Temper Selection Starts After the Alloy Number

A purchase order that says 6063 aluminum extrusion identifies the alloy chemistry, but it does not identify the material’s mechanical state. The number 6063 tells the mill which magnesium, silicon, iron, copper, and other element limits apply. The temper after the dash—such as T4, T5, or T6—tells you how the profile was cooled, heat treated, aged, and sometimes mechanically relieved.

That suffix is not a minor production detail. In a heat-treatable 6xxx alloy, cooling and aging control the formation of magnesium-silicon strengthening particles. Two profiles can both meet the 6063 chemistry limits and still have substantially different yield strength, ductility, forming behavior, and distortion risk. Before requesting a price, [specify the right temper](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/6063-aluminum-extrusion-specify-the-right-temper-before-you-order_n1033) alongside the alloy, profile, and governing specification.

## T5 and T6 Are Different Processing Histories

6063-T5 is cooled from an elevated-temperature shaping process and then artificially aged. For many extrusions, controlled cooling takes place as the profile leaves the press, followed by an aging-oven cycle. T5 is common in architectural profiles, trim, light framing, and other parts where moderate strength, efficient extrusion, dimensional control, and surface quality are important.

6063-T6 is solution heat treated, quenched, and artificially aged. The solution step dissolves the magnesium and silicon into the aluminum matrix before rapid cooling retains them in a condition that can produce a stronger precipitate structure during aging. Depending on the equipment and profile, the solution treatment may be achieved through a controlled press quench or a separate furnace reheat and quench.

The distinction matters because T6 is not simply T5 with a longer oven cycle. Artificial aging alone cannot create a valid T6 condition if the required solution treatment and quench were not completed first. A supplier offering to upgrade T5 by adding another aging cycle should be asked exactly which temper and certified properties will result.

### The Minimum Numbers Matter More Than Catalog Averages

Typical property values show what a production run often achieves. They are useful for comparison, but they are not the same as guaranteed design values. For extruded 6063 sections up to 0.500 inch thick, ASTM B221 minimums commonly cited for T5 are 22 ksi ultimate tensile strength and 16 ksi yield strength. The corresponding T6 minimums are 30 ksi ultimate tensile strength and 25 ksi yield strength for sections up to 1.000 inch thick. T5 minimums decrease to 21 ksi ultimate and 15 ksi yield for sections from 0.501 to 1.000 inch.

Typical figures can make the difference look different again. A common reference point is about 27 ksi ultimate and 21 ksi yield for 6063-T5, compared with about 35 ksi ultimate and 31 ksi yield for 6063-T6. The proper design comparison, however, is the applicable specification minimum. At the same thin-section thickness, the T6 minimum yield strength is 9 ksi higher than the T5 minimum—a major difference when yielding governs.

A drawing that uses 31 ksi as though it were guaranteed can overstate capacity. A drawing that assumes only 16 ksi when the supplier is required to deliver T6 may be unnecessarily heavy. The alloy number alone cannot resolve either problem.

## Choose the Temper by the Failure Mode

Temper selection becomes much clearer when the part’s governing constraint is identified before the alloy is ordered.

### When Yielding or Connection Strength Governs

T6 earns its additional processing when the profile has to resist a known bending stress, concentrated bearing load, fastener load, or other condition where material strength limits the design. As a simple screening example, suppose the applicable material check requires at least 18 ksi yield strength. A 6063-T5 minimum of 16 ksi would not clear that screen, while a 6063-T6 minimum of 25 ksi would. The final design still needs the correct allowable-stress method, safety factors, local buckling checks, and connection analysis, but the temper choice could change the result.

T6 is especially useful when the section cannot be made deeper or thicker because of packaging, clearance, or interface limits. It can provide more strength within the same envelope. That benefit has to be weighed against lower ductility, more demanding quench control, and the possibility of greater residual stress in thin or complex profiles.

### When Deflection Governs

A stronger temper does not automatically make a profile stiffer. The elastic modulus of 6063-T5 and 6063-T6 is essentially the same, about 68.9 GPa or 10,000 ksi. Elastic deflection depends primarily on that modulus and the cross-sectional moment of inertia.

If a long 6063-T5 rail sags under load, switching to T6 in the same geometry will produce nearly the same initial elastic deflection. A deeper section, a closed box shape, a shorter span, or an added support usually solves that problem more effectively. T6 can increase the load required to cause permanent yielding, but it does not transform a flexible shape into a rigid one.

This distinction prevents a common specification error: choosing T6 to compensate for an undersized profile when stiffness—not yield strength—is the real design limit.

### When the Part Must Be Formed After Extrusion

T5 and T6 are poor starting points for severe post-extrusion forming compared with softer tempers. 6063-T4 is solution heat treated and naturally aged, so it retains more formability. O temper is softer still and is reserved for applications where severe forming takes priority over immediate strength.

A profile that will be bent, flared, swaged, or roll formed should be ordered in a condition that supports that operation. If the finished part needs higher strength, the forming sequence and subsequent aging treatment must be defined and validated. Simply ordering T6 and forcing it around a tight radius can produce cracking, excessive springback, or inconsistent dimensions.

The final temper also needs to be clear. A part may be supplied in T4 for forming but require a controlled aging step afterward. The supplier, fabricator, and designer should agree on which properties apply before forming and which properties are required in the completed part.

### When Finish and Profile Complexity Govern

6063 is widely selected for visible architectural work because it extrudes easily and usually produces a clean, uniform surface for anodizing. T5 is often a practical choice for thin walls, slots, trim, window components, and light framing when the load is moderate.

The temper does not eliminate die lines or surface defects. Anodizing follows the underlying metal surface, so die lines, scratches, and uneven flow remain visible. Surface quality depends on billet quality, die condition, press control, handling, and pretreatment as well as alloy selection.

T6 can still be correct for a complex profile when the strength calculation requires it, but the quench route deserves closer review. A rapid or uneven quench can leave residual stress and cause bow, twist, or dimensional movement. Thin hollows and asymmetric sections are more sensitive than simple solid bars. The supplier should confirm how the profile will be cooled, stretched, straightened, and aged before the temper is accepted.

## Thermal Performance Does Not Make T6 the Automatic Choice

Heat sinks provide a useful example of why temper decisions should follow the actual requirement. Published typical datasets place 6063 thermal conductivity near 200 to 209 W/m-K, with some values showing T5 slightly above T6. That difference is modest compared with the effect of fin height, fin spacing, airflow, base thickness, and contact resistance.

If the heat sink has enough mechanical strength in T5, the easier extrusion route and slightly higher typical conductivity may support that choice. If the assembly needs T6 strength for mounting loads or structural integration, the small conductivity difference may not matter. Neither decision should be made from a generic label such as high-performance aluminum. The profile geometry and service conditions determine which property controls.

## Welding Can Change the Temper Decision at the Joint

A mill certificate for 6063-T6 describes the unwelded extrusion, not the strength of every location after fabrication. Welding heats the metal beside the joint and partly reverses the strengthening treatment. The heat-affected zone in T5 or T6 material can lose roughly 30 to 50 percent of its strength, depending on the process and thermal exposure.

A welded assembly therefore cannot use the full T6 property everywhere without checking the reduced values for the heat-affected zone. The Aluminum Design Manual and the applicable welding design standard provide the appropriate basis for welded members. Critical joints may need larger sections, sleeves, gussets, or bolted connections rather than relying on the original temper at the weld.

This does not make T6 useless in a welded assembly. It means the temper decision must account for where the strength will actually survive. A T6 member away from the joint and a reduced-strength welded zone may require separate checks.

## Do Not Treat T1 or T52 as Casual Substitutes

T1 is cooled from an elevated-temperature shaping process and naturally aged. It generally provides lower guaranteed properties than T5 because it does not receive the same artificial-aging step.

T52 is a separate temper designation with its own standard definition and controlled property range. It should appear on a drawing only when a governing specification, customer standard, or engineering requirement calls for it. A supplier should provide the written temper definition and certified property range rather than treating T52 as a convenient synonym for T5.

The absence of a temper on an order should never give the supplier permission to select T1, T4, T5, T52, or T6 based only on inventory or convenience.

## Turn the Decision Into an Enforceable RFQ

A complete purchase-order line states the alloy, temper, standard, thickness category, and certification requirement. A useful T5 example would read:

> 6063-T5 aluminum extrusion per ASTM B221, current edition; profile drawing revision C; applicable wall thickness 0.125–0.500 inch; certify the specification minimum tensile and yield properties; provide a lot-traceable mill test report; no temper substitution without written approval.

A T6 order should add the process information needed to verify the more demanding treatment:

> 6063-T6 aluminum extrusion per ASTM B221, current edition; supplier to identify whether solution treatment is achieved by controlled press quench or separate furnace treatment; provide lot-traceable tensile certification and the artificial-aging practice; no substitution with T5, T4, T52, or another temper without written approval.

The RFQ should also state whether dimensions apply before or after finishing, which surfaces are cosmetic, whether the part will be bent or welded, and which property values are required in the completed assembly. These details prevent a supplier from quoting an easily extruded T5 profile when the design actually depends on T6 strength—or from quoting an unnecessarily expensive T6 profile for a part that only needs a clean architectural finish.

### Questions That Expose an Unclear Temper

Ask the supplier the following before approving samples or comparing prices:

1. Is the quotation for 6063-T5, 6063-T6, or another defined temper?
2. Are the listed tensile and yield values typical production results or specification minimums?
3. Which ASTM B221 thickness category applies to the thinnest and thickest portions of the profile?
4. For T6, is solution treatment achieved on the press or in a separate furnace?
5. How are quench uniformity, stretching, straightness, and artificial aging monitored?
6. Does the test certificate trace the tensile result to the actual production lot?
7. Will the profile be formed, welded, or heavily machined after delivery?
8. Which properties are valid for the finished part after those operations?

Two quotes that both say 6063 are not comparable until these questions have the same answers. A lower-priced T5 quote is not an economical alternative to a required T6 condition, and a T6 quote is not better value if the part will be bent or if deflection controls the design.

## The Purchasing Rule

The alloy number answers what the metal is. The temper answers what condition the metal is in and which properties the supplier must certify.

Choose T4 or O when forming comes first. Choose T5 when extrusion efficiency, surface quality, dimensional control, and moderate strength meet the service need. Choose T6 when verified minimum strength is essential and the profile, quench route, and fabrication process can tolerate the added treatment. If deflection governs, change the geometry instead of assuming T6 will make the section stiffer. If welding governs, design the heat-affected zone rather than relying on the mill temper alone.

Printing the temper after 6063 is a small change to a drawing, but it closes one of the largest gaps between a quoted extrusion and the material the design actually requires.
