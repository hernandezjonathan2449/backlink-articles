# Aluminum Extrusion Cost: The Per-Pound Quote Is Not the Price

## The Per-Pound Number Is a Starting Point, Not a Cost

The most common mistake in aluminum extrusion procurement is comparing two numbers that look identical but cover different work.

A supplier may quote $1.85 per pound for an ex-works, mill-finish profile before cutting, packaging, freight, or import charges. Another may quote $2.40 per pound for a finished, cut-to-length profile delivered to the buyer’s facility. The second quote looks more expensive, but the comparison is not valid.

The real purchasing question is not which supplier has the lowest per-pound number. It is:

> Which supplier delivers conforming, usable parts at the lowest total cost for the required quantity, quality, and delivery date?

Aluminum is commonly sold by weight, so per-pound pricing is useful for screening raw material and press conversion costs. It becomes unreliable as the final decision metric once tooling, finishing, machining, yield, logistics, and quality risk enter the order.

## One Pound Can Represent Four Different Products

A quote can use the phrase per pound while referring to several different points in the production process:

- **Input pound:** The billet or metal charged into the extrusion process. This basis may include material that becomes startup scrap or process waste.
- **Press-exit pound:** The aluminum that leaves the die before cutting, finishing, and secondary operations.
- **Shipped pound:** The net weight packaged and sent to the customer, which may exclude rejected pieces or production scrap.
- **Accepted pound:** The weight of parts that pass dimensional, cosmetic, and functional inspection and can actually be used.

Those are not interchangeable denominators. A supplier quoting on input pounds can appear cheaper than one quoting on accepted output even when both have similar manufacturing economics.

A transparent [extrusion cost breakdown](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-cost-per-pound-exposed-what-vendors-won-t-tell-you_n963) is useful only when every line has a defined unit, scope, and delivery boundary. Without those definitions, a per-pound figure creates false precision.

The same problem appears when a buyer needs finished parts rather than raw profile. A lightweight profile may require more length, more rack space, more cuts, and more drilled holes to produce the same shipment weight as a heavy structural shape. The metal weight is equal, but the work content is not.

## The Fully Loaded Cost Formula

A practical comparison should convert every charge into an order total and then divide that total by accepted output:

> True cost per accepted pound = total material, conversion, tooling, finishing, fabrication, logistics, quality, and carrying costs ÷ accepted pounds delivered

The numerator should include the costs required to receive usable product at the specified destination:

1. **Metal and press conversion:** Separate the aluminum index or billet price from the conversion charge. The conversion charge covers press time, energy, labor, setup, and overhead.
2. **Tooling:** Include die design, die manufacture, sampling, revisions, storage, and replacement terms. A simple die may cost hundreds of dollars, while hollow or complex dies can cost several thousand dollars.
3. **Finishing:** Anodizing, powder coating, PVDF, brushing, polishing, and other surface treatments should be priced against the actual finish specification, not just a generic finish label.
4. **Secondary operations:** Sawing, drilling, punching, CNC machining, bending, deburring, and assembly often create per-piece or setup costs that do not scale neatly with pounds.
5. **Logistics:** Include packaging, inland freight, ocean or air freight, insurance, port handling, customs brokerage, duties, and delivery to the final facility.
6. **Quality and yield:** Account for inspection, testing, rejected parts, rework, replacement shipments, and expected scrap.
7. **Carrying and schedule costs:** Long lead times can require additional inventory, financing, storage, or expedited freight when demand changes.

Yield requires special care. If a supplier has already included a scrap surcharge in the quote, adding the same scrap percentage again can overstate cost. If the quote covers all production input but only 98% becomes acceptable output, dividing by accepted pounds correctly captures the economic effect without adding a second scrap charge.

## Geometry Makes Per-Pound Finishing Misleading

Surface treatments expose one of the biggest weaknesses in weight-based pricing: finishing often follows surface area, length, rack loading, or handling time rather than metal weight.

Consider two illustrative profiles, each ordered at 1,000 pounds:

- A heavy channel weighing 2 pounds per foot produces approximately 500 linear feet.
- A thin-wall decorative profile weighing 0.8 pounds per foot produces approximately 1,250 linear feet.

The metal weight is identical, but the second order contains 2.5 times as much linear material. If both profiles are cut into 5-foot pieces, the heavy shape produces about 100 pieces while the thin profile produces about 250 pieces. If every piece requires two drilled holes, the light profile requires 500 holes compared with 200 for the heavy one.

An anodizing line may handle the two orders differently because the thin profile occupies more rack length and may expose more surface area per pound. The machining department will also spend more time on the lighter profile because there are more individual pieces. A simple per-pound finishing allowance can hide that difference.

Profile geometry also affects extrusion yield. Thin walls, narrow webs, sharp corners, and demanding dimensional tolerances can require slower press speeds and more frequent process adjustments. A complex profile may therefore cost more even when its alloy and finished weight match a simpler catalog shape.

The practical rule is simple: when finishing or fabrication is significant, request pricing per piece, per foot, or per operation in addition to pricing per pound. The most informative cost model may use several units at once.

## A Worked Quote Comparison

Consider an illustrative order for 10,000 nominal pounds of the same 6063-T5 custom profile. The specification requires clear anodizing, saw cutting, and two drilled holes per finished piece.

### Supplier A: Regional Production

The quote contains these charges:

- Press and alloy cost: $2.40 per pound, or $24,000
- Clear anodizing: $0.60 per pound, or $6,000
- Cutting and drilling: $0.35 per pound, or $3,500
- Custom die: $1,000
- Packaging and delivery: $1,500
- Receiving inspection: $600

The order total is $36,600. With a 98% accepted yield, the buyer receives 9,800 usable pounds.

**True cost: $36,600 ÷ 9,800 = $3.73 per accepted pound.**

### Supplier B: Imported Production

The imported supplier quotes a lower press price, but the cost scope is different:

- Ex-works press and alloy cost: $1.85 per pound, or $18,500
- Clear anodizing: $0.55 per pound, or $5,500
- Cutting and drilling: $0.30 per pound, or $3,000
- Custom die: $2,000
- Ocean, port, and inland freight: $4,500
- Assumed import and compliance charges: $3,500
- Third-party inspection: $1,200
- Additional inventory and coordination cost: $800

The order total is $39,000. With a 94% accepted yield, the buyer receives 9,400 usable pounds.

**True cost: $39,000 ÷ 9,400 = $4.15 per accepted pound.**

The imported quote looks $0.55 per pound cheaper at the press stage, but it ends up $0.42 per accepted pound higher. The import and compliance line in this example is an assumption, not a universal tariff rate; actual charges depend on classification, origin, trade rules, and the shipment structure.

If the buyer needs 10,000 accepted pounds rather than 10,000 production pounds, Supplier A would need to run approximately 10,204 pounds at a 98% yield. Supplier B would need approximately 10,638 pounds at a 94% yield. The additional input affects metal, finishing, freight, and production capacity, widening the gap further.

If each finished part weighs 2 pounds, Supplier A delivers about 4,900 usable parts at $7.47 per part. Supplier B delivers about 4,700 usable parts at $8.30 per part. The per-part calculation makes the commercial result easier to understand because production teams consume parts, not abstract pounds.

## Tooling Is a Fixed Cost Disguised as a Per-Pound Cost

Custom dies create another reason to distrust an isolated per-pound number. Tooling is largely fixed, while the order quantity is variable.

A $1,500 die adds very different amounts at different volumes:

- At 500 pounds, the die adds $3.00 per pound.
- At 1,000 pounds, it adds $1.50 per pound.
- At 2,000 pounds, it adds $0.75 per pound.
- At 5,000 pounds, it adds $0.30 per pound.
- At 10,000 pounds, it adds $0.15 per pound.

Suppose the press and finishing price is $2.25 per pound. The effective first-run cost becomes $5.25 per pound at 500 pounds but only $2.40 per pound at 10,000 pounds.

This does not mean custom extrusion is automatically expensive. It means the forecast volume must be credible. Spreading a die across three years of expected purchases is appropriate only if those purchases are contractually likely. A projected volume that never materializes leaves the first order carrying the full tooling burden.

A quote should also state who owns the die, who pays for replacement, whether revisions are included, and whether the tooling charge is credited after a volume threshold. A tooling credit is economically valuable only when the credit terms are written clearly and the buyer can realistically reach the required volume.

## How to Make Vendor Quotes Comparable

The fastest way to improve quote quality is to require the same commercial structure from every supplier. The request for quotation should ask for both the order total and the normalized cost per accepted part or accepted pound.

Require these points in writing:

1. **Price basis:** Identify whether the quote is EXW, FOB, CIF, delivered, or another Incoterm, and name the delivery destination.
2. **Metal basis:** State the alloy, temper, aluminum index, regional premium, currency, and price-adjustment formula.
3. **Quantity basis:** Specify whether pounds refer to billet, press output, shipped product, accepted product, or a minimum billing quantity.
4. **Yield assumption:** State the expected scrap and rejection rate and identify who pays when yield falls below the assumption.
5. **Tooling:** Separate die design, die manufacture, sampling, revisions, ownership, storage, and replacement charges.
6. **Finish:** Define anodize thickness and color, powder-coat specification, gloss, pretreatment, masking, and batch minimums.
7. **Fabrication:** List every cut, hole, bend, CNC feature, deburring step, tolerance, and setup charge on a per-piece or per-order basis.
8. **Quality:** Define inspection methods, certificates, sampling plans, rejection procedures, and rework responsibility.
9. **Logistics:** Include packaging, freight, insurance, duties, brokerage, port fees, and expected delivery date.
10. **Commercial validity:** State quote expiration, minimum order quantity, lead time, escalation clauses, and terms for repeat orders.

A useful internal worksheet then follows four steps:

- Convert every supplier charge into dollars for the same order quantity.
- Estimate accepted output using one consistent yield assumption or each supplier’s documented yield.
- Divide the complete order cost by accepted pounds and accepted pieces.
- Test the result under realistic changes in metal price, freight, yield, and delivery timing.

The result is not an attempt to force every supplier into the same manufacturing method. It is a way to ensure that different methods are compared at the same point of value: conforming product delivered where it is needed.

## The Metric That Should Govern the Award

Per-pound pricing remains useful for an early screen. It can reveal an obviously uncompetitive press conversion charge or show the effect of a commodity-price change. It should not govern the final award when the order includes custom tooling, thin-wall geometry, decorative finishing, machining, imported logistics, or meaningful rejection risk.

For the final decision, use:

> Cost per accepted part = total landed order cost ÷ conforming parts received

Use cost per accepted pound as a secondary measure when parts vary in size or when the customer’s production process consumes material by weight. Use cost per foot when length drives installation, and use cost per operation when drilling, cutting, or machining dominates labor.

The winning supplier is the one with the lowest reliable cost for conforming product delivered on schedule—not necessarily the one with the lowest number beside the words per pound. Once the denominator, scope, yield, and delivery boundary are made explicit, the apparent bargains and genuine savings become much easier to distinguish.
