# Aluminum Alloy Selection for Custom Extrusion Parts

## The Best Alloy Is Chosen at the End of the Process

One of the most expensive mistakes in custom extrusion is approving an alloy from a data sheet before defining what the finished part must do. A reliable [final-part extrusion plan](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/custom-aluminum-extrusion-profiles-from-alloy-selection-to-final-part_n998) starts with the installed component and works backward to the billet.

6063 and 6061 are both heat-treatable 6000-series aluminum alloys, but they optimize different compromises. The alloy affects how metal flows through the die, how thin or complex the profile can be, how the surface responds to anodizing, how the part behaves during machining and welding, and how much risk enters the production schedule.

Typical T6 reference values show the contrast clearly: 6063 has tensile strength around 30,000 psi and a Brinell hardness near 73, while 6061 reaches roughly 45,000 psi and a Brinell hardness near 95. Actual minimums vary with temper, section thickness, product standard, and supplier process. Those numbers matter, but they do not answer the more important question: which alloy produces the most reliable finished part?

## Why Strength-First Selection Fails

Tensile strength is only one measure of performance. It helps predict resistance to yielding and permanent deformation, but it does not automatically determine stiffness, surface quality, corrosion life, thermal behavior, or manufacturing cost.

Both 6063 and 6061 have an elastic modulus of roughly 10 million psi. That means changing from 6063 to 6061 does not dramatically increase elastic stiffness. If a long extrusion bends too far under load, a deeper section, additional rib, shorter span, or better support may solve the problem more effectively than a stronger alloy.

Consider a bracket carrying a 400-pound load with a strict deflection limit. If the bracket fails because it bends elastically, 6061 may provide little improvement because the modulus is similar. If it fails because a bolt hole locally yields or a threaded feature strips, 6061's higher strength and hardness may be the correct solution. The failure mode—not the largest number on the material certificate—should drive the selection.

The same logic works in reverse. Choosing 6061 for a decorative trim profile because it is stronger can create unnecessary difficulty when the part has thin walls, narrow channels, or a visible anodized face. The additional strength may never be used, while the profile becomes harder to extrude and more difficult to finish consistently.

## What 6063 and 6061 Are Really Optimizing

### 6063: Geometry, Appearance, and Corrosion Resistance

6063 is often called the architectural alloy because it produces a clean surface and flows well through demanding dies. It is commonly selected when the profile includes:

- Thin walls or closely spaced features
- Hollow chambers and narrow channels
- Decorative or visible surfaces
- Anodized finishes requiring consistent color
- Outdoor exposure and strong corrosion resistance
- Heat sink fins or other geometry where extrusion flow matters

Its superior extrudability gives the die designer more room to balance metal flow through uneven sections. That can reduce twisting, distortion, die correction, and surface defects in complex shapes. It is especially useful when the profile must combine structural ribs, gasket grooves, lens channels, and visible trim in one cross-section.

6063 also responds particularly well to anodizing. Anodizing converts the aluminum surface into an integral oxide layer rather than covering it with a thick film. Variations in the base alloy and extrusion surface can therefore remain visible after treatment. The relatively clean chemistry of 6063 generally produces a more uniform decorative result than 6061.

The tradeoff is lower strength. A 6063 profile may need a larger section, thicker wall, or better load distribution than a comparable 6061 design. That is not a weakness in every application; it is a design condition that must be checked against actual loads, deflection, fastener forces, and safety factors.

### 6061: Load, Hardness, and Machining

6061 earns preference when the finished component must tolerate concentrated loads, repeated fastening, substantial machining, or structural stress. It is common in:

- Machinery brackets and supports
- Structural frames and bases
- Transport and industrial components
- Machined housings
- Parts with tapped holes and high bolt preload
- Assemblies where strength matters more than decorative appearance

The alloy's higher strength and hardness can improve resistance to local deformation around holes, slots, and joints. Its machinability is also generally better suited to drilling, tapping, milling, and other secondary operations than softer architectural grades.

The manufacturing tradeoff appears in the extrusion press and at the finishing line. A complex thin-wall or hollow section that runs comfortably in 6063 may require slower production, more careful die balancing, or geometric changes in 6061. The same CAD model is not automatically equally manufacturable in both alloys.

6061 can be anodized, but it is usually not the first choice for a highly uniform decorative finish. Its alloying elements can produce more variation in color and appearance. That limitation becomes much less important when the part is hidden, powder coated, or used in a functional assembly where the finish is primarily protective.

### Temper Is Part of the Alloy Decision

Specifying only 6063 or 6061 leaves out a major part of the requirement. Temper determines how the alloy achieves its mechanical properties and can affect dimensional behavior, straightness, heat treatment, and cost.

A 6063-T5 part is not interchangeable with a 6063-T6 part, and neither should be treated as equivalent to 6061-T6. The correct temper depends on the required strength, section geometry, post-extrusion treatment, and service conditions. Temper should appear on the drawing and purchase specification alongside the alloy, not as an assumption left to the supplier.

## How Alloy Choice Propagates Through the Finished-Part Chain

The alloy decision reappears at every production handoff. Evaluating only the extrusion press hides the downstream consequences that often determine the real cost and reliability of the part.

### Die Feasibility

6063's more forgiving flow characteristics support intricate cross-sections with thin walls, narrow openings, and closely spaced features. 6061 can produce custom shapes successfully, but the die may require different bearing lengths, pocket design, press speed, or wall proportions to maintain balanced flow.

This is why changing alloys after a die has been designed can create unexpected expense. A die proven in 6063 may not deliver the same dimensions or surface quality in 6061. The supplier may need a new trial, die correction, slower production speed, or a revised section. Alloy substitution should be treated as an engineering change rather than a purchasing change.

### Surface Treatment and Appearance

Anodizing magnifies the importance of surface quality. Scratches, die lines, streaks, and chemistry-related color variation can remain visible after the oxide layer is formed. For window frames, trim, furniture, signage, and other exposed parts, 6063 often minimizes this risk.

Powder coating is more forgiving because the cured polymer layer conceals many minor substrate differences. It does not make poor extrusion quality acceptable, but it reduces the premium placed on decorative anodizing response. A hidden machine frame and a visible bronze-anodized window profile should not be held to the same alloy logic.

### Machining, Fastening, and Joining

6061's higher hardness and favorable machinability can make it the better choice for parts that receive many holes, threads, pockets, or bearing seats. The benefit is especially clear when a profile is used as a near-net-shape blank for a machined bracket or housing.

6063 can also be machined, but its lower hardness may require different tooling, feeds, and chip-control practices. If only one small area receives machining, the entire profile should not automatically be upgraded to 6061 without checking whether a local insert, thicker boss, or revised fastener could solve the problem more economically.

Welding adds another constraint. Heat-affected zones can lose a substantial portion of the original T6 strength unless the joint and post-weld condition are engineered accordingly. A high-strength alloy selected for an unwelded bracket may not retain the expected performance after welding. The joining method belongs in the original alloy decision.

### Service Environment

Both alloys offer useful corrosion resistance, but 6063 generally has an advantage in exposed architectural environments because of its chemistry and finishing response. Salt, moisture, industrial chemicals, ultraviolet exposure, and trapped water can still overwhelm an unsuitable coating or poorly designed joint.

Alloy, temper, surface treatment, drainage, and contact with dissimilar metals work together. Specifying 6063 does not eliminate the need for a suitable anodize or coating system, and specifying 6061 does not automatically make an outdoor part unsuitable. The final environment determines which combination is acceptable.

## Three Finished-Part Decisions That Produce Different Answers

### An Anodized Window Mullion

A window mullion may contain hollow chambers, gasket grooves, drainage paths, and a large visible face. Its requirements are dimensional consistency, weather resistance, a clean anodized appearance, and sufficient strength for the calculated glazing and wind loads.

6063 is often the logical starting point because its extrusion behavior and anodizing response align with the dominant requirements. If structural calculations show that 6063 in the selected temper and section is adequate, choosing 6061 solely for its higher tensile strength adds a property the part may not need. If the calculations demand more capacity, the solution could be a deeper section, additional wall thickness, another temper, or a different alloy—not an automatic material swap.

### A Machined Motor Bracket

A motor bracket may be hidden behind a powder-coated enclosure, drilled and tapped for multiple fasteners, and exposed to vibration and concentrated bolt loads. Decorative anodizing is irrelevant, while resistance to local deformation and reliable machining are important.

6061-T6 is often the stronger candidate. Its higher strength and hardness can support threaded features and bolt preload with less risk of damage. A simpler cross-section may also make its lower extrusion flexibility unimportant. Selecting 6063 because it is easier to extrude would not create value if the finished bracket then needs thicker bosses, inserts, or extra machining to achieve the required joint strength.

### An LED Heat Sink Housing

An LED housing can combine thin cooling fins, a lens-retention channel, mounting provisions, and a visible anodized exterior. Here, profile geometry, thermal transfer, finish, and assembly all compete for priority.

6063 often performs well when fin density, smooth extrusion flow, and anodized appearance dominate. 6061 becomes more attractive when the mounting face carries high clamp loads or the housing requires extensive machining. A well-engineered design may keep the efficient 6063 profile and isolate a high-load fastener with a reinforced boss or insert, provided thermal and mechanical testing support that approach.

These examples do not make 6063 an architectural-only alloy or 6061 an industrial-only alloy. They show why the final part's dominant requirement matters more than a general-purpose label.

## Total Part Cost Matters More Than Alloy Price

Material price is visible on a quote, but alloy-related production risk is often hidden in tooling changes, scrap, finishing rejects, slow press speeds, and extra machining. Production reviews repeatedly show the same pattern: the nominal alloy premium is easy to see, while the cost of a poor fit appears later as rework and schedule disruption.

Tooling illustrates the effect of volume. A hypothetical $2,000 die adds $0.20 per part across 10,000 parts, but $4 per part across only 500 parts. If the wrong alloy forces a die correction or creates a high rejection rate, that cost can overwhelm a small difference in billet price.

A part-level comparison should include:

- Alloy and temper cost
- Die design, manufacture, and possible correction
- Extrusion speed and expected yield
- Heat treatment and straightening
- Anodizing, powder coating, or other finishing
- CNC cutting, drilling, milling, and tapping
- Inspection, packaging, and material certification
- Scrap, rework, and expected production stability

A heavier 6063 design can be less expensive than a 6061 design if it runs faster, finishes more consistently, and avoids machining or die problems. Conversely, 6061 may be the lowest-cost choice when it enables a thinner load-bearing section or eliminates a separate machined bracket. The correct comparison is the approved, assembly-ready part—not the price per pound of unfinished extrusion.

## A Production-Ready Method for Alloy Selection

A disciplined [part-first alloy workflow](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/custom-aluminum-extrusion-profiles-from-alloy-selection-to-final-part_n998) turns the 6063-versus-6061 decision into a measurable engineering exercise.

1. **Define the dominant failure mode.** Record load, deflection, fatigue, thread strength, thermal limits, corrosion exposure, appearance requirements, and expected service life. Separate properties that are mandatory from preferences that are merely desirable.

2. **Map the finished part's process chain.** Identify the smallest wall, deepest cavity, tightest radius, visible surfaces, machining features, welded joints, coating system, and critical mating dimensions. A complex section that is easy to finish may still be difficult to extrude, and a strong alloy may still fail an appearance requirement.

3. **Compare candidate alloys using the same geometry.** Ask the extrusion supplier whether 6063 and 6061 can both run through the proposed section, what temper is realistic, how the die would change, and what production speed or yield to expect. Do not compare one alloy in a thin section with another in a heavier section and call the result equivalent.

4. **Evaluate production-intent samples.** Check dimensions after extrusion, aging, machining, and finishing. Inspect anodized or coated faces under agreed lighting, test fit with mating parts, verify mechanical certification when required, and perform load or thermal tests that represent actual use. A bare mill-finish sample cannot validate the final appearance or assembly behavior.

5. **Lock the requirement into the specification.** The drawing and purchase order should state alloy, temper, finish, critical tolerances, acceptable visual limits, secondary operations, inspection requirements, and change-control rules. A supplier should not substitute 6061 for 6063, or change temper, simply because both materials are described as aluminum extrusion.

If both alloys meet the functional requirements, select the one with the better combination of production yield, supplier capability, finish reliability, lead time, and lifecycle cost. The highest theoretical strength is not a tie-breaker unless strength is actually the limiting requirement.

## The Rule That Holds Up in Production

> Choose the alloy that protects the finished part's dominant requirement with the fewest downstream compromises. Use 6063 when shape, appearance, corrosion resistance, or extrusion flow leads the decision. Use 6061 when load, hardness, machining, or joint performance leads it. Treat strength as a requirement to verify, not a default reason to upgrade.

The right alloy is the one that remains appropriate after the profile is extruded, heat-treated, finished, machined, assembled, and placed in service. That final-part perspective prevents over-specification, exposes hidden manufacturing risks early, and produces a more defensible cost and performance decision than alloy selection based on a single tensile-strength number.
