# Powder Coating Specification for Aluminum Extrusions: Define the Whole System

## The finish is a system, not a color

The most consequential decision in a powder coating specification is not the RAL number. It is whether the requirement describes the complete coating system from bare aluminum to maintained service.

Powder coating is often written as though it were a single material. It is actually a sequence: extrusion surface, cleaning, conversion treatment, powder resin, electrostatic deposition, cure, handling, inspection, and maintenance. A failure in any link can defeat the others. A flawless color match over poor pretreatment is still a failed finish.

On real extrusion projects, the recurring failure pattern is easy to miss: a black 6063-T5 profile for a dry indoor enclosure and a black 6063-T5 profile for a coastal facade can look identical when they leave the plant. After exposure to UV, salt, condensation, and cleaning chemicals, their performance can diverge sharply. The durable profile is not necessarily the one with the thickest film. It is the one whose entire process was matched to its service environment and then verified.

> A powder-coating callout should function as a performance contract: define the exposure, coating class, pretreatment, cure, acceptance tests, and maintenance conditions.

That viewpoint changes how a buyer evaluates a powder-coated aluminum extrusion. The question is no longer whether a supplier can apply black powder. The question is whether the supplier can document a repeatable system for this alloy, this cross-section, this environment, and this expected service life.

## Why a short finish note creates long-term risk

A drawing note such as the following appears precise:

- Aluminum extrusion: 6063-T5
- Finish: black powder coat
- Thickness: 60 μm

It still leaves the decisions that control durability open:

- Is the resin epoxy, hybrid, standard polyester, super-durable polyester, or a fluoropolymer system?
- Which AAMA, Qualicoat, or GSB performance class applies?
- What cleaning and conversion treatment will be used?
- Is 60 μm a local minimum, an average, or merely a nominal target?
- How will the applicator prove that the metal reached the required cure temperature?
- Which surfaces must be coated, and where are rack marks acceptable?
- What visual, adhesion, color, gloss, and corrosion criteria determine acceptance?
- What maintenance obligations and warranty conditions apply?

The word black identifies a color family, not a weathering class. An epoxy can provide excellent interior chemical resistance and still chalk quickly in sunlight. A super-durable polyester is better suited to many exterior architectural applications, while a premium fluoropolymer system may be justified where long-term color and gloss retention carry a high replacement cost. All three can be supplied in a similar black.

Film thickness is equally easy to misunderstand. A single reading on a broad outside face cannot represent a deep slot, a sharp edge, an internal corner, or an area close to a rack contact. A nominal 60 μm target can conceal a thin region that is vulnerable to corrosion or an excessive build that interferes with an assembly. A generic number without a measurement plan is not a quality requirement; it is an invitation to interpret.

A short note also transfers change control to the supplier. If the powder brand, pretreatment chemistry, or oven settings change, the purchaser may have no objective basis for rejecting the result. A complete specification makes those changes visible.

## Start with the exposure, not the color catalog

A reliable specification begins with an exposure profile. At minimum, establish:

- whether the part is indoors, outdoors, or intermittently exposed;
- ultraviolet intensity, building orientation, and expected color sensitivity;
- distance from saltwater and exposure to salt-laden air;
- industrial pollutants, alkaline wash-down, solvents, oils, or cleaners;
- abrasion, impact, and handling during installation;
- condensation, standing water, crevices, and drainage conditions;
- required appearance life and the cost of access for replacement; and
- the profile geometry, including slots, cavities, tight corners, and machined areas.

Three common scenarios show why the same finish name cannot serve as the specification.

**Dry interior machine enclosure.** If the profile remains indoors and is exposed to cutting fluids or cleaning chemicals but little sunlight, an epoxy or epoxy-polyester hybrid may be a rational choice. The required tests should emphasize adhesion, chemical resistance, hardness, and cleanability rather than long-term Florida weathering.

**Urban exterior window or door system.** The profile sees UV, rain, temperature cycling, and routine cleaning. A durable exterior polyester system qualified to the project’s architectural standard is often the cost-effective baseline. The requirement should address color and gloss consistency across elevations, not simply initial appearance.

**High-visibility coastal facade.** Salt deposits, intense sunlight, windborne grit, and expensive access for rework raise the consequence of premature fading or corrosion. A higher architectural performance class, a controlled pretreatment process, careful edge and joint design, and a documented maintenance schedule may justify a premium fluoropolymer or other approved weatherable system. The standard should be named explicitly rather than left to the applicator.

These are not automatic recipes. A dry indoor part can be over-engineered with an expensive exterior system, while a budget exterior powder can be dangerously under-specified for a marine facade. Exposure defines the performance target; color is selected after that target is clear.

## Name the chemistry and the performance class together

Coating chemistry and performance classification answer different questions. Chemistry describes how the film is expected to behave. A standard defines how the selected system must perform under specified tests. The specification needs both.

- **Epoxy** is strong on adhesion and chemical resistance, making it useful for many interior industrial parts. Its poor ultraviolet stability generally rules it out for unprotected exterior aluminum.
- **Epoxy-polyester hybrid** improves light stability over straight epoxy while retaining useful interior chemical resistance. It is often appropriate for indoor equipment, fixtures, and furniture that may see incidental daylight, but it should not be treated as a premium outdoor system by default.
- **Standard polyester** is a common choice for general exterior and consumer applications where weathering demands are moderate.
- **Super-durable polyester** extends exterior weathering performance and is frequently used for architectural windows, doors, railings, and facade components when the project standard permits it.
- **Fluoropolymer systems** are considered when color retention, gloss retention, and long-term appearance are more important than minimum first cost. The exact resin technology and qualification must be documented; a label such as PVDF or fluoropolymer is not, by itself, proof of compliance.

For a North American project, the specification may call for AAMA 2603, AAMA 2604, or AAMA 2605 depending on the required performance level. International work may use Qualicoat or GSB requirements. These designations are not interchangeable marketing adjectives. The contract should identify one required class, the applicable edition, the approved product or product family, and the evidence of qualification.

Do not write AAMA 2604 or AAMA 2605 as an open-ended alternative unless the design team genuinely accepts the performance difference. Do not specify AAMA 2605 while allowing an unqualified generic powder. A performance class applies to a defined coating system under controlled application conditions; it does not automatically validate every powder in a supplier’s inventory.

## Treat pretreatment as part of the finished product

Aluminum arrives at the coating line with an oxide surface, extrusion lubricants, handling oils, dust, and sometimes residues from storage or machining. Powder cannot compensate for contamination. The pretreatment sequence creates the interface that lets the cured film adhere and helps slow corrosion beneath scratches and cut edges.

A typical sequence includes:

1. alkaline cleaning or another approved degreasing step;
2. rinsing;
3. deoxidizing or controlled etching where required;
4. a conversion coating;
5. final rinsing and drying; and
6. transfer into powder application without uncontrolled delay.

The exact chemistry can be chromate-based or chrome-free, depending on the specification, regulations, customer requirements, and approved coating system. Chrome-free does not automatically mean better, and chromate is not automatically acceptable. What matters is that the pretreatment is compatible with the powder and controlled within the supplier’s documented process window.

A serious specification asks for more than the phrase pretreat before coating. It identifies or approves the pretreatment system and requires evidence such as:

- bath concentration, pH, temperature, and time controls;
- rinse-water quality or conductivity limits where applicable;
- conversion-coating coverage;
- maximum time between pretreatment and powder application;
- adhesion and corrosion testing appropriate to the selected standard; and
- records that link the pretreatment batch to the finished extrusion lot.

The conversion layer is thin enough to disappear visually, yet it can decide whether a defect remains local or becomes underfilm corrosion. A premium powder over a weak conversion layer is still a weak coating system.

Before releasing drawings, a [powder-coating specification guide](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/powder-coated-aluminum-extrusions-essential-points-before-you-specify_n971) can help organize the technical questions, but the contractual requirement should still name measurable evidence rather than rely on a supplier’s general process description.

## Control cure by metal temperature, not the oven display

Curing is a thermal event in the aluminum, not a number on the oven controller. Powder technical data sheets normally specify a metal-temperature window and the time the metal must remain within that window. Many architectural powders use a metal-temperature range around 160–205°C, or 320–401°F, for roughly 10–25 minutes at the target temperature, but those figures are examples rather than a universal recipe.

A thin-walled profile and a heavy section on the same rack do not heat at the same rate. The oven may display 200°C while a thick boss or enclosed section has not reached the powder’s minimum metal temperature. The reverse problem can occur on a thin wall: it reaches the target quickly and stays hot long enough to overbake while the heavy section is still catching up.

The specification should therefore require:

- the selected powder’s cure schedule and acceptable window;
- a thermal profile using thermocouples on representative thin, thick, and difficult sections;
- verification after meaningful changes in line speed, load, rack orientation, or oven settings;
- records of time above the required metal temperature; and
- a defined cure check, such as the manufacturer’s approved solvent-resistance, hardness, or other verification method.

Under-cure can leave a soft film with weak chemical resistance and poor adhesion. Over-cure can shift color and gloss, reduce flexibility, or make the film more brittle. An oven-air temperature log without a metal profile does not prove either condition was controlled.

## Specify thickness as a range with a measurement plan

Powder films for aluminum extrusions commonly fall in the broad range of about 50–125 μm, or 2–5 mils, but the correct range belongs to the product technical data sheet and the governing standard. More thickness is not automatically more protection.

Excessive build can create orange peel, runs, edge accumulation, dimensional interference, and poor appearance at corners. Insufficient build can leave edges, recesses, and low-deposition areas vulnerable. Texture powders and metallic finishes can also make visual judgment unreliable, so instrument readings matter.

A usable thickness requirement answers four questions:

1. What is the target or allowable range?
2. Is the lower limit a local minimum or an average?
3. Where will readings be taken on the actual profile?
4. How many readings represent each production lot?

A calibrated eddy-current gauge can measure cured nonconductive coating on aluminum without cutting the part. Readings should cover broad faces, edges, recesses, and other locations identified during first-article approval. If the powder technical data calls for 60–100 μm, write that range and define the sampling method. Do not copy a generic 60 μm value onto every profile or assume a high average proves coverage in a slot.

Thickness also interacts with assembly. A coated sliding interface, threaded hole, press fit, or gasket seat may need masking, post-machining, or a different tolerance allowance. The finish specification must be coordinated with the mechanical drawing.

## Qualify the actual extrusion geometry

A flat test panel can confirm that a powder color looks right and that a coating line can produce a reasonable film. It cannot prove that a deep T-slot, hollow section, sharp corner, or narrow internal channel will coat acceptably.

Electrostatic application is efficient, but recessed geometry can create a Faraday-cage effect that reduces powder deposition. Edges may receive a different film build from broad faces. Rack contacts remain uncoated or may require a controlled repair. Internal surfaces may not be fully accessible at all.

The drawing and coating specification should identify:

- visible A-surfaces and less-visible B-surfaces;
- required coating on internal cavities, slots, and tube interiors;
- acceptable rack locations and maximum rack-mark size;
- areas to be masked or plugged;
- minimum edge and corner coverage where it affects corrosion;
- machining, drilling, tapping, and cutting sequence; and
- treatment of exposed metal at post-coating cuts or repairs.

The first article should use the production die, alloy and temper, actual extrusion surface, planned rack orientation, approved powder, pretreatment line, and intended cure schedule. A separately supplied flat coupon is useful for color reference, but it is not a substitute for the worst-case profile.

Machining order deserves special attention. Machining before coating can protect cut edges but may complicate masking and dimensional control. Machining after coating can damage the film and expose bare aluminum. Threads, holes, mating faces, and cut ends need a written treatment rather than an assumption that a touch-up aerosol will restore factory performance.

## Turn quality into an acceptance plan

The strongest specification divides evidence into preproduction approval, first-article approval, and production-lot control.

### Before production

Require the supplier to submit:

- the named powder product, resin family, color, gloss, texture, and technical data sheet;
- the required AAMA, Qualicoat, or GSB qualification evidence;
- pretreatment chemistry and process-control limits;
- cure schedule expressed as metal temperature and time;
- proposed dry-film thickness range and measurement method;
- a physical color and gloss master sample; and
- a written change-control process prohibiting unapproved powder or pretreatment substitutions.

A RAL or brand color code is a useful starting point, but it does not settle gloss, texture, metallic orientation, or batch-to-batch variation. A physical master made with the intended powder and approved on the actual extrusion is more reliable than a screen image or catalog chip.

### At first article

Inspect the first production pieces under defined lighting and viewing distance. Check:

- uniform color, gloss, and texture;
- runs, craters, pinholes, blisters, exposed metal, and contamination;
- film thickness on representative faces, edges, and recesses;
- adhesion using an applicable method, such as cross-cut testing where the surface texture permits;
- cure using the powder manufacturer’s approved verification method;
- masking, rack marks, holes, threads, and assembly interfaces; and
- dimensional fit after coating.

Instrument standards such as ASTM D7091 for thickness, ASTM D523 for gloss, ASTM D2244 for color difference, and ASTM D3359 for adhesion can be useful when they match the coating and project requirements. The standard should be named in the inspection plan, not added after a dispute begins.

### During production

Maintain traceability for powder lot, pretreatment conditions, cure profile, extrusion lot, and operator or line identification. Repeat thickness and visual checks at a defined frequency. Re-profile the oven when the line speed, rack density, profile mass, or loading pattern changes.

Weathering and corrosion tests generally qualify the coating system rather than replace production inspection. Salt-spray hours can reveal weaknesses, but they do not predict every outdoor failure mode or establish a field service life by themselves. A credible approval package combines the required standard, product data, process records, and actual production checks.

## Make the warranty and maintenance match the specification

A warranty that says only workmanship is covered may provide little protection against premature fading, chalking, adhesion loss, or corrosion. Ask the supplier to state separately:

- coverage for color change and gloss loss;
- coverage for chalking, adhesion failure, and underfilm corrosion where applicable;
- service duration and the exposure assumptions behind it;
- responsibilities for pretreatment, installation damage, sealants, fasteners, and cut edges;
- required cleaning frequency and approved methods; and
- the repair process and materials for field damage.

Maintenance is part of the performance system. A mild urban exterior may be cleaned about every six months, while salt-laden or industrial environments often require cleaning every three months or at another interval set by the coating manufacturer. Mild detergent, clean water, and soft tools are normally safer than abrasive pads, strong solvents, or aggressive alkaline cleaners. Sheltered recesses deserve special attention because rain does not wash accumulated deposits from them.

Field touch-up should never be treated as identical to factory coating. A two-component repair product may provide a durable repair when properly prepared, but the repair area can still differ in gloss or texture. Define who approves the material, how damaged edges are prepared, how exposed aluminum is sealed, and when a part must be stripped and recoated instead.

The economic reason for this discipline is simple: on an installed facade, access equipment, removal, reinstallation, and schedule disruption can cost far more than the original powder. A modest premium for a documented system and a first-article check can prevent a much larger rework decision.

## A model performance-based coating clause

For a high-UV exterior storefront, a usable requirement could read as follows:

> Aluminum extrusions for the high-UV exterior storefront shall receive a super-durable polyester powder coating qualified to AAMA 2604. The applicator shall submit the named product technical data sheet, pretreatment process, approved color and gloss sample, and coating qualification evidence before production.
>
> Aluminum shall be cleaned, deoxidized as required, conversion-coated with a chemistry compatible with the selected powder, rinsed, dried, and coated within the documented process window. Dry-film thickness shall remain within the powder manufacturer’s specified range; a project target of 60–100 μm shall apply only if it matches that product’s technical data.
>
> Cure shall be verified by metal-temperature profiling on representative thin and heavy sections against the powder manufacturer’s schedule. The first article shall use the production extrusion, rack orientation, powder batch, pretreatment line, and cure settings. Acceptance shall include visual inspection, calibrated thickness readings, adhesion and cure checks, and color and gloss comparison with the approved master. No powder or pretreatment substitution shall occur without written approval.

For a marine, high-rise, or especially visible facade, the first sentence would need a higher project-required performance class if the exposure analysis warrants it. The important act is changing the performance requirement, not merely adding more powder or choosing a darker color. Writing AAMA 2604/AAMA 2605 as a supplier option simply returns the central design decision to the coating line.

## The question that exposes an incomplete specification

Before awarding the work, ask the supplier one practical question:

**How will you prove that this coating system will perform on this exact extrusion in this exact environment?**

A credible answer names the powder product, performance class, pretreatment controls, metal-temperature cure profile, film-thickness sampling plan, first-article method, traceability records, warranty, and maintenance instructions. A weak answer points to a color chart, a generic process photograph, or a single salt-spray number.

The best powder coating specification is not the one with the longest chemistry label or the highest nominal film thickness. It is the one that leaves no critical handoff implicit. When exposure, pretreatment, chemistry, cure, geometry, inspection, and maintenance are written as one connected system, powder-coated aluminum extrusions become predictable to quote, verify, install, and maintain. Color is then the easy part.
