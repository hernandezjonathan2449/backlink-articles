# Architectural Aluminum Extrusions: Thermal Break Design

## The Hidden Specification Is Continuity

A recurring failure pattern in facade submittals is a specification that is exact about the metal but vague about the envelope. It may name 6063-T5, a 2.5-inch sightline, an anodized finish, and a wall thickness, yet never state how much heat the installed frame may transmit or how cold its interior surface may become. The result can be a perfectly made extrusion that performs poorly as a building component.

That distinction belongs in [architectural extrusion specifications](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/architectural-aluminum-extrusions-a-few-specification-secrets-architects-rarely-share_n1011): the extrusion is only one part of a thermal, structural, air, and water-control system. Aluminum mullions, transoms, pressure plates, glass edges, gaskets, fasteners, anchors, and surrounding insulation all participate in the heat path. If any of those parts creates a continuous metal shortcut, the thermal break on the profile may deliver much less than its catalog description suggests.

> The reliable specification starts with the path of heat and moisture, then works backward to alloy, geometry, and fabrication.

## Why Alloy and Temper Do Not Answer Thermal Performance

6063 and 6061 are not interchangeable for every structural or aesthetic purpose. 6063 generally extrudes more easily and produces a cleaner anodized surface. 6061 usually offers higher strength for engineered load-bearing members, although it can be less forgiving for visible finishes. T5 and T6 describe different strengthening histories and affect mechanical performance. Those are important decisions, but they do not solve the envelope problem.

At room temperature, common architectural aluminum alloys conduct heat at roughly 170 to 200 W/m·K, depending on alloy and temper. A glass-filled polyamide thermal isolator may test around 0.25 to 0.5 W/m·K, depending on its formulation and test conditions. The exact values vary, but the relationship does not: the isolator is hundreds of times less conductive than the aluminum.

Changing an unbroken mullion from 6063-T5 to 6061-T6 may increase strength and alter machinability while leaving the primary conductive bridge intact. A 3-inch unbroken 6061-T6 section and a 3-inch unbroken 6063-T5 section are both still metal pathways from the interior to the exterior. By contrast, a correctly designed thermally broken 6063 section may provide better envelope performance even when it is not the strongest material option.

The alloy still needs to satisfy structural calculations, finish requirements, corrosion exposure, and fabrication limits. It simply should not be used as a shortcut for thermal qualification. A temper designation tells the engineer how the metal may behave under load; it does not tell the building owner how warm the inside of the frame will remain on a cold morning.

## A Thermal Break Works Only When the Path Stays Broken

A thermal break is not a decorative insert added for marketing. It is an interruption in a load-bearing, weather-exposed assembly. Mechanically locked polyamide strips and poured-and-debridged systems must maintain alignment, transfer the required shear between interior and exterior sections, resist creep, and accommodate differential thermal movement over the service life.

The most common bypasses occur at:

- Mullion-to-transom intersections and corner keys
- Pressure plates, end dams, and glazing pockets
- Steel reinforcement, anchors, and fasteners that cross the isolation plane
- Sills, thresholds, door frames, and sliding-track interfaces
- Slab-edge attachments and perimeter closures
- Glass spacers and setting details that control edge-of-glass temperatures

A thermal break can be continuous along a straight mullion and still be defeated at its corner, sill, or pressure plate. The metal bridge may be small in area, but condensation starts at the coldest local surface rather than at the average frame temperature.

At 70°F and 40% relative humidity, indoor air has a dew point of about 45°F. If the interior aluminum at a corner falls below that temperature during a 10°F outdoor condition, condensation can form even when the center of the glass remains dry. In a 75°F interior maintained at 60% relative humidity, the dew point is approximately 60°F, leaving much less margin for error.

A thermal break can raise the interior surface temperature, but only when the corner, sill, glass edge, and attachment details are included in the analysis. A claim that a profile is thermally broken is therefore incomplete without knowing where the break begins, where it ends, and what crosses it.

## Put the Requirement Into Numbers

The basic heat-flow relationship is straightforward: **Q = U × A × ΔT**. Heat flow increases with the assembly U-factor, the area, and the temperature difference between indoors and outdoors.

Consider an illustrative 10,000-square-foot glazed facade evaluated at a 50°F temperature difference. If one whole-assembly design has a U-factor of 0.60 Btu/h·ft²·°F and another has a U-factor of 0.35, conductive heat flow would be approximately 300,000 Btu/h for the first design and 175,000 Btu/h for the second. The difference is 125,000 Btu/h before accounting for solar gains, infiltration, operating schedules, and other building loads.

Those values are examples rather than universal targets. Their purpose is to show why the performance number must apply to the actual assembly. A frame U-factor, center-of-glass U-factor, and whole-window U-factor are different measurements. Combining them without identifying the boundary conditions can make a high-performing product look better than the installed facade actually is.

A defensible architectural aluminum specification should identify, as applicable:

- The maximum frame and whole-assembly U-factor
- The minimum interior surface temperature or condensation-resistance criterion at the design temperature and indoor humidity
- The glazing, spacer, gaskets, and mullion-to-transom ratio used in the calculation
- Air leakage at the project test pressure
- Water penetration performance and structural deflection
- Thermal bridges at corners, anchors, slab edges, sills, and transitions

For U-factor work, request an NFRC-based calculation or a project-specific thermal model using the actual frame and glazing configuration. For air, water, and structural performance, use the applicable project testing procedures, such as ASTM E283, ASTM E331, and ASTM E330. These tests address different failure modes; a strong structural test does not prove low heat flow, and a low U-factor does not prove water tightness.

## Geometry Is Where Thermal and Structural Design Meet

An extrusion is a structural section and a heat-flow diagram at the same time. Its depth, cavities, wall thickness, flange locations, and isolator position determine both stiffness and thermal behavior.

A 2.5-inch sightline tells the designer the visible face width, but not the distance between the thermal isolators, the depth of the frame, the size of the glazing cavity, or the amount of metal connecting the interior and exterior. Two profiles with the same sightline can have very different U-factors and deflection performance.

For a simple rectangular section, moment of inertia increases approximately with the cube of depth. That is why adding depth in the correct direction can improve mullion stiffness far more effectively than simply changing from a lower-strength to a higher-strength temper. At the same time, adding a large uninterrupted metal web can create a stronger but colder frame.

Consider a 12-foot mullion under a substantial wind load. Replacing 6063-T5 with 6061-T6 may improve material strength, but reducing the section depth from 3 inches to 2.5 inches can reduce stiffness and leave less room for a properly positioned thermal isolator. The substitution is not automatically equivalent because the structural and thermal geometries have both changed.

The thermal isolator also has to work with the structural design. It may not carry the entire mullion load, but its connections still need to transfer the forces imposed by wind pressure, glass weight, pressure plates, and building movement. A supplier should be able to show the crimp, lock, or pour detail; isolator width; shear-transfer method; and expected movement.

This is where a custom die can be more valuable than a standard profile. A well-designed cavity can accommodate the isolator, glazing gasket, drainage route, fastener access, and reinforcement without creating avoidable bridges. A cheaper standard section that requires adapter plates or field-added metal may have a lower purchase price but a weaker thermal path and more difficult installation.

## How to Write a Thermal-Break Specification That Survives Review

1. **Set the boundary conditions first.** Identify the climate, outdoor design temperature, indoor temperature, expected relative humidity, wind pressure, span, exposure, glazing type, and installation position. A thermal target without those conditions is difficult to verify.

2. **State measurable assembly outcomes.** Specify a maximum U-factor for the actual frame or fenestration assembly, not just the glass. Add a condensation-resistance or minimum interior surface-temperature requirement where the project has high humidity or cold-weather exposure.

3. **Require a section-specific thermal path.** Show the sightline, frame depth, isolator width, cavity geometry, pressure plate, gaskets, drainage, fasteners, and reinforcement. A product name or generic profile number is not enough.

4. **Require continuity at interfaces.** The thermal barrier should remain effective through mullions, transoms, corners, sills, thresholds, pressure plates, and perimeter connections. Any metal that crosses the isolation plane should be identified and included in the calculation.

5. **Select alloy and temper for their actual jobs.** Use 6063 when extrusion quality and visible finish are priorities, and use 6061 or another engineered alloy when strength calculations require it. Verify the specified alloy and temper with material certifications, but do not treat those certifications as thermal evidence.

6. **Require matching documentation.** Thermal results should use the specified glass, spacer, gaskets, frame geometry, and fastener pattern. Structural calculations, air and water test reports, and a project mockup may also be necessary for complex curtain walls.

7. **Control substitutions by performance.** An alternate should demonstrate equal or better assembly U-factor, condensation resistance, structural capacity, air leakage, and water penetration under comparable boundary conditions. Matching the alloy or visible sightline alone does not establish equivalence.

A concise performance-oriented clause might read:

> Provide a thermally broken aluminum framing system sized for the stated climate, indoor humidity, span, and design loads. The thermal barrier shall remain continuous through mullions, transoms, corners, sills, and pressure-plate interfaces. Submit section drawings, structural calculations, and thermal results for the specified glass, spacer, gaskets, and fastener pattern. Substitutions shall demonstrate equal or better whole-assembly U-factor and condensation resistance; alloy designation or sightline alone shall not establish equivalence.

## A Bid Substitution Exposes a Weak Specification

Assume a project establishes a 3-inch thermally broken curtain wall with a whole-assembly U-factor no greater than 0.35 Btu/h·ft²·°F under a defined glazing package. A lower-priced bid proposes a 2.5-inch 6063-T6 profile with the same anodized color and a line item labeled thermal barrier.

The proposal may appear close, but several questions remain:

- Is the stated U-factor for the complete assembly or only the frame?
- Does the calculation use the same glass, spacer, pressure plate, and gasket arrangement?
- Are the mullion-to-transom joints, corners, anchors, and fasteners included?
- Does the shallower profile preserve the required moment of inertia and glazing capacity?
- Is the thermal isolator mechanically locked and rated for the system loads?
- Were air and water tests performed on the same configuration?

If those questions are unanswered, the alternate is a new system rather than a cheaper version of the specified system. It may save on aluminum weight or tooling while shifting cost into HVAC capacity, field modifications, condensation repairs, or rejected submittals.

The most reliable bid review compares measured outcomes under matching conditions. A supplier that cannot provide the relevant section drawing and performance evidence has not yet demonstrated equivalence, regardless of how familiar the alloy designation looks.

## Specify the Path, Then Choose the Profile

Alloy, temper, finish, wall thickness, and sightline still matter. They answer questions about strength, extrusion quality, corrosion resistance, appearance, and fabrication. Thermal-break design answers a different question: whether the installed frame interrupts heat and moisture movement where the building needs it to.

Once the heat path is drawn, design tradeoffs become clearer. A deeper profile may improve stiffness while providing room for a wider isolator. A custom cavity may reduce thermal bridging without sacrificing drainage or hardware access. A stronger alloy may satisfy a load case, while 6063 remains the better choice for a highly visible anodized surface. Each decision has a defined purpose instead of being used as a substitute for whole-assembly performance.

The governing rule is simple: **specify the path of heat, verify the installed assembly, and use the alloy label as supporting information rather than the performance guarantee.**
