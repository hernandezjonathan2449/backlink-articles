# AAMA Coating Standards for Aluminum Extrusions: Choose by Exposure

## AAMA coating standards are a risk decision, not a quality ranking

A request for AAMA 2605 often gets treated as shorthand for the safest possible finish. That shortcut can be expensive and may still fail to address the actual hazard. AAMA coating standards are performance specifications for organic coatings on aluminum extrusions and panels. They establish requirements for weathering, appearance retention, adhesion, and other physical properties; they do not assign a guaranteed service life to a particular facade.

The sounder decision is not Which number is highest? It is: What exposure will this profile see, how much appearance change is acceptable, and what will correction cost if the finish underperforms? An [AAMA standards guide](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/painting-services-for-aluminum-extrusions-aama-standards-decoded_n990) helps decode the tiers, but the decisive step is translating those definitions into a building-specific exposure profile.

### What the three tiers actually mean

The designations are best understood as minimum performance tiers rather than a universal quality ranking:

- **AAMA 2603:** The basic tier, generally suited to interior applications and exterior areas protected from prolonged sunlight and moisture. It includes a shorter South Florida weathering benchmark and does not impose the same stringent post-exposure appearance-retention demands as the higher tiers.
- **AAMA 2604:** The high-performance or super-durable tier. It uses a five-year South Florida exposure benchmark and tighter requirements for properties such as color retention, gloss retention, chalking, and erosion. It is commonly appropriate for general exterior architecture where the finish must remain stable but future access is manageable.
- **AAMA 2605:** The superior-performing tier. It uses a ten-year South Florida exposure benchmark and the most demanding retention requirements of the three. Many systems used to meet it are fluoropolymer-based, although the designation describes verified performance rather than a particular brand or resin chemistry.

The one-year, five-year, and ten-year periods are qualification test durations, not countdowns to field failure. AAMA 2605 does not promise that a facade will look unchanged for exactly ten years, and AAMA 2604 does not mean a finish will fail after five. The test periods create a common basis for comparing systems under severe, standardized outdoor exposure.

The standards originated under the American Architectural Manufacturers Association and are now maintained within the Fenestration and Glazing Industry Alliance. Architectural drawings and specifications still commonly use the established AAMA numbers, so the current edition should be identified whenever a project is bid.

## An AAMA rating is not an all-hazards score

AAMA weathering performance is crucial, but it is only one part of an exterior coating decision. A project can meet AAMA 2605 and still experience problems if the dominant risk is outside what the rating models or if the production process is poorly controlled.

Coastal chloride exposure is a good example. Salt-laden air can attack cut ends, fasteners, joints, and damaged areas even when the exposed coating has excellent color retention. A high AAMA tier does not eliminate the need for appropriate pretreatment, drainage, isolation of dissimilar metals, edge protection, and project-specific corrosion requirements.

The same principle applies near chemical plants, swimming pools, wastewater facilities, and industrial washdown areas. A weathering qualification does not automatically establish resistance to concentrated acids, alkaline cleaners, solvents, or repeated chemical immersion. Those projects may need chemical-resistance data, immersion testing, abrasion testing, or a coating system selected specifically for the contaminant.

This distinction prevents a common misdiagnosis: upgrading from AAMA 2604 to AAMA 2605 when the actual problem is inadequate pretreatment or chemical attack. The higher number may improve UV and color performance, but it cannot compensate for a failure mode that the specification was never designed to control.

## Build the exposure profile before selecting the tier

A reliable finish specification starts with the conditions surrounding the aluminum, not with a preferred product name. Record the following factors before requesting a quote:

- **Solar exposure and orientation:** South- and west-facing elevations in the Northern Hemisphere typically receive intense afternoon sun. Dark finishes can absorb more heat, and color changes are more visible on large, uninterrupted curtainwall surfaces.
- **Moisture and contaminants:** Wind-driven rain, condensation, standing water, salt spray, road salts, and airborne pollutants create very different risks from a dry, ventilated interior.
- **Temperature cycling:** Large daily or seasonal temperature swings can stress the coating and the assembly. Dark colors and large profiles may experience greater thermal movement than light-colored, sheltered components.
- **Geometry:** Horizontal ledges and recesses collect dirt and water differently from vertical faces. A covered soffit, exposed mullion, and rooftop screen on the same building should not automatically be treated as one exposure class.
- **Maintenance access:** A finish on a ground-floor storefront can be inspected and repaired with ordinary equipment. The same finish on a twentieth-floor facade may require swing stages, lifts, traffic control, and extensive scheduling.
- **Appearance consequence:** A minor gloss or color shift may be acceptable on a concealed support but unacceptable on a branded entrance or high-visibility facade.
- **Operating environment:** Chlorides, industrial chemicals, abrasive dust, frequent cleaning, and physical contact may require additional performance requirements beyond the AAMA weathering tier.

Once these factors are documented, the appropriate AAMA level usually becomes clearer. Without them, a project is likely to default either to the cheapest finish or to AAMA 2605 everywhere, neither of which is a technically defensible method.

## Three common applications show why the tier must match the risk

### AAMA 2603 can be rational for sheltered work

Consider interior office partitions, indoor retail framing, equipment-room trim, or an exterior soffit protected by a deep overhang. These profiles may see little direct UV, limited rain, and regular access for cleaning or replacement. If appearance demands are moderate, AAMA 2603 can provide an appropriate balance of protection and cost.

Specifying 2605 for that application may buy performance the component cannot use. The extra weathering capability does not create a meaningful benefit if the profile is indoors and can be refinished without scaffolding. The exception is an interior space with strong ultraviolet exposure through large glazing, aggressive cleaning chemicals, or unusually high appearance requirements. Those conditions should be recorded rather than assumed away.

### AAMA 2604 is often the practical exterior center

A two-story commercial building, municipal facility, or accessible residential project may experience full sun and rain but still allow economical inspection and maintenance. AAMA 2604 often fits this middle ground: stronger exterior weathering performance than a basic coating, without automatically paying for the highest tier on every component.

That choice still depends on color, geography, and exposure. A light-colored profile under a covered walkway is not equivalent to a dark profile on an unshaded west elevation. A building near the ocean may need enhanced corrosion protection even when 2604 is sufficient for UV retention. The correct specification may be AAMA 2604 plus explicit marine pretreatment and edge-treatment requirements.

### AAMA 2605 earns its premium when correction is difficult

A high-rise curtainwall with dark gray profiles, strong south and west exposure, and limited maintenance access presents a different risk. A slight color shift can become obvious across a continuous elevation, while a future recoat may require specialized access equipment and coordination with building occupants.

In that case, AAMA 2605 is not simply a luxury upgrade. It is a way to reduce the probability and consequence of appearance-related failure. The same logic applies to landmark buildings, corporate headquarters, airports, and other projects where inconsistent color or gloss would carry reputational or operational costs.

### AAMA 2605 still may not answer an aggressive-environment problem

An industrial enclosure near a chemical process, a marine structure exposed to saltwater splash, or an assembly subject to repeated abrasive cleaning may need more than a superior weathering classification. The specification should add the relevant chemical, corrosion, abrasion, and adhesion tests instead of treating 2605 as a universal shield.

The key question is whether the failure mode is fading and chalking, or whether it is corrosion, chemical breakdown, impact, or edge failure. The AAMA tier should be selected for the first category and supplemented for the others.

## One building may need more than one AAMA tier

A single building-wide requirement is convenient for purchasing, but it can be technically blunt. A project may contain several exposure zones:

- Interior framing and protected soffits may use AAMA 2603.
- Accessible, moderately exposed exterior framing may use AAMA 2604.
- Unshaded, high-visibility elevations or difficult-to-access upper levels may use AAMA 2605.

Using multiple tiers requires coordination. Adjacent components with the same nominal color can show differences in gloss, texture, or color if they use different coating systems. Production samples should therefore be reviewed together under consistent lighting. If a continuous visual field crosses two tiers, the project may reasonably standardize that field on one system even when a lower tier would be adequate in a less visible zone.

This zone-based approach is more precise than specifying 2605 for every profile and more protective than assigning 2603 to an entire building because some components are sheltered.

## Lifecycle cost explains the right choice better than coating price

The initial coating quote is only one part of the economic decision. A useful comparison is:

**Total finish cost = initial coating premium + handling and logistics + maintenance access + recoating cost + failure risk.**

Consider an illustrative 10,000-square-foot project. If the quoted premium for moving from 2604 to 2605 is $0.35 per square foot, the added initial cost is $3,500. That may be difficult to justify for interior partitions. On a high-rise facade, however, one future inspection or recoat campaign involving specialized access, traffic control, and labor could exceed that premium by several multiples.

The reverse is also true. If a component is easy to remove, inexpensive to repaint, and shielded from severe weather, paying for a premium tier may produce no proportional reduction in risk. Lifecycle reasoning does not always favor the highest standard; it favors the standard that minimizes total exposure-related cost.

## Make the selected tier enforceable in the specification

A vague requirement such as painted aluminum with AAMA-quality finish leaves too many variables open. The coating manufacturer, resin family, pretreatment, film thickness, cure schedule, alloy, and color can all affect the result.

A stronger project requirement can read like this:

> The aluminum extrusions shall receive an organic coating system meeting the current edition of AAMA 2604 for the approved color and gloss. The supplier shall identify the coating product, resin family, pretreatment system, compatible alloy and temper, dry-film thickness range, cure requirements, production testing, and warranty coverage.

The same language can be adapted for AAMA 2603 or 2605. The important point is that the AAMA number appears alongside the actual system and exposure requirements.

The RFQ should also identify:

- The exposure zone and building elevations covered by the requirement.
- The approved color, gloss level, texture, and sample-approval process.
- Any marine, chemical, abrasion, or cleaning requirements beyond weathering.
- The aluminum alloy and temper, especially when multiple extrusion alloys are involved.
- Pretreatment chemistry and required handling before coating.
- Minimum and maximum dry-film thickness, rather than assuming thicker is always better.
- Oven cure or other process requirements based on the coating manufacturer's instructions.
- Required quality records, test methods, and warranty exclusions.
- Treatment of drilled holes, cut ends, field damage, and touch-up materials.

An AAMA claim from a coating manufacturer is not the same as a production record for the profiles delivered to the job. The specification should identify who is responsible for demonstrating compliance and what documentation must accompany each production batch.

## Verify the system, not just the label

A coating system can be qualified on paper and still underperform when the production line has poor pretreatment control, inconsistent film build, or incomplete cure. Verification should therefore connect the standard to the finished extrusion.

Before full production, approve representative samples from the actual alloy, profile geometry, pretreatment line, coating product, and cure process. A small color chip cannot reveal how a complex extrusion handles recessed surfaces, edges, and hanging points.

During production, useful checks include:

- Dry-film thickness readings at multiple locations on representative profiles.
- Color and gloss comparison against the approved production sample under consistent lighting.
- Cure and oven records tied to the batch, when required by the coating system.
- Adhesion or other performance checks performed according to the project quality plan.
- Inspection of edges, recesses, and handling points where coverage can vary.
- Documentation of repairs and field-cut protection, since an AAMA classification does not automatically extend to uncoated modifications made after finishing.

These controls do not replace the AAMA qualification tests. They confirm that the supplied profiles were produced using the system that was specified and that obvious deviations are caught before installation.

## The practical rule for choosing an AAMA standard

Use AAMA 2603 when exposure is low, the component is sheltered or interior, and correction is simple. Use AAMA 2604 for ordinary exterior exposure where reliable weathering performance is needed and maintenance access remains manageable. Use AAMA 2605 when UV exposure, color sensitivity, visibility, or access difficulty makes premature appearance change especially costly.

Then add separate requirements for hazards the AAMA tier does not fully model, particularly marine corrosion, industrial chemicals, abrasion, and field modifications. Finally, verify the actual coating system and production controls rather than accepting the tier as a marketing label.

The strongest aluminum finishing specification is not a blanket demand for AAMA 2605. It is a documented match between exposure and performance tier, supported by a coating system, process controls, and maintenance plan that address the real cost of failure.
