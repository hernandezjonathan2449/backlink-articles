# Polycarbonate Thermal Cracking: Why Aluminum Extrusions Need Movement

## The Real Reason Polycarbonate Cracks

Polycarbonate sheets almost never fail because the material is too weak. They fail because the installation turns a moving sheet into a trapped sheet. Once that happens, thermal expansion has only one place to go: into the edge, the fastener, or the seal line.

That is the central insight behind aluminum extrusions for polycarbonate sheets. A good extrusion is not a clamp in the ordinary sense. It is a controlled movement joint that supports the panel, protects the edge, and still leaves room for the sheet to expand and contract without being crushed. [engineered extrusion systems](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusions-for-polycarbonate-sheets-stop-thermal-cracking-forever_n816) work because they are designed around movement, not just retention.

### Expansion Is the Load You Cannot Ignore

Polycarbonate moves a lot more than most framing materials. A practical rule from field guides is about 1/8 inch of movement per 3 feet of sheet at a 100°F temperature swing. That sounds small until the panel gets longer.

A 12-foot panel can want roughly 1/2 inch of total movement over the same temperature change. A 6-foot panel needs about 1/4 inch at each edge. Dark bronze or other heat-absorbing sheets need even more room because they run hotter in direct sun.

If both edges are captured tightly in a narrow channel, the sheet does not politely stay put. It buckles, bows, or pushes stress into the weakest point.

The failure usually starts in the morning after installation looks perfect. The sheet is cut square, the screws are tight, and the sealant bead is neat. Then the sun heats the roof. The panel grows. The frame does not. The result is compressive stress at the edges and tensile stress at any drilled point or abrupt stop.

### Why the Extrusion Geometry Matters More Than the Brand Name

A polycarbonate sheet needs room to move in three places:

- along its length
- across its width
- at every point where a fastener or gasket touches it

That is why profile geometry matters so much. A profile that is too shallow, too narrow, or too rigid turns the aluminum into a vise. A true [thermal movement profile](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusions-for-polycarbonate-sheets-stop-thermal-cracking-forever_n816) gives the panel enough clearance to slide a little without losing weather resistance.

The best systems do three things at once:

1. **Distribute pressure instead of concentrating it.**  
   The sheet edge should bear against a broad surface, not a knife edge or a point load.

2. **Seal without locking.**  
   Gaskets, caps, and seals need to compress just enough to block water and dust while still allowing slip.

3. **Accommodate expansion with geometry, not force.**  
   Oversized holes, slotted attachments, and deeper channels are not optional details. They are the reason the assembly survives temperature swings.

If a profile only works when the sheet is forced into place, it is not a good fit. It is a future crack line.

### The Common Mistake: Treating Every Attachment Point as Fixed

The most expensive errors I see are not dramatic. They are small decisions repeated across an entire roof or wall.

A few examples:

- a channel sized for the nominal thickness but not for thermal movement
- screws driven too tightly through the sheet
- no edge clearance at the ends of long runs
- rigid sealant applied where a flexible seal was needed
- panels cut exactly to the opening instead of slightly short

Each one seems harmless. Together they create a system that has no slack. Once the temperature rises, the sheet tries to expand and finds itself locked at every boundary.

The visible damage is familiar: whitening around fasteners, edge crazing, popping sounds in heat, leaks after the first seasonal cycle, and eventually cracks radiating from stress points. By the time the crack appears, the real problem has already been present for weeks or months.

### What I Look for in a Crack-Resistant Installation

A sound installation feels slightly forgiving during dry fit. The panel should seat without hammering. It should not be so loose that it rattles, but it should not be jammed so tightly that insertion requires force.

I use the same basic checks on every project:

- the sheet can move a little inside the channel
- the edge is protected from direct contact with hard metal
- the fastener holes are not acting like anchors
- the gasket compresses evenly across the contact line
- the run has enough allowance for the hottest expected day

That last point matters more than most people think. A panel installed on a cool spring morning may look perfect, but the real test comes under summer sun. If the installation can survive the hottest day without forcing the sheet against the frame, it usually survives the rest of the year as well.

### The Real Job of Aluminum

Aluminum’s value here is not just corrosion resistance or appearance. Its deeper advantage is that extrusion lets the profile be shaped around controlled movement. The metal gives you the precision of a formed channel, but the design can still leave room for the panel to breathe.

That is why the right framing concept prevents thermal cracking so effectively. It does not try to defeat expansion. It makes expansion harmless.

In practice, that means the best assemblies are deliberately a little more generous than a first-time installer expects. The profile is not a grip in the aggressive sense; it is a guide. The panel is held, sealed, and supported, but never squeezed into silence.

Once that difference is understood, most recurring failures become easy to explain. The sheet did not suddenly become brittle. The weather did not suddenly become extreme. The installation simply removed the movement the material required.

If the frame lets the panel move, the panel stays intact. If the frame traps the panel, cracking is only a matter of time.
