# Aluminum Extrusion Cutting: Control Chip Load for Burr-Free Ends

## The Hidden Variable Behind a Clean Aluminum Cut

Clean aluminum extrusion ends are often treated as a blade-selection problem. The blade matters, but the deeper variable is chip load: the amount of aluminum each tooth removes during one pass.

A square, low-burr end appears when every tooth takes a small, predictable bite while the profile remains completely stable. If the tooth suddenly takes too much material, it grabs and tears the edge. If it takes too little, it rubs instead of cutting, creating heat, smeared aluminum, and welded chips. Most cutting defects are different symptoms of that same instability.

A broader [aluminum cutting guide](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/how-to-cut-aluminium-extrusion-square-burr-free-ends-every-time_n1042) can help compare saws and blades, but chip-load control explains why those choices work.

> Clean aluminum cuts come from a stable chip load: enough bite to cut, not enough to grab, and no movement when the tooth exits the profile.

On a production saw, burr-free usually means no raised hook, rolled lip, or torn edge that interferes with assembly. A tiny edge break after cutting may still be specified. The objective is to make the saw produce a controlled edge rather than relying on heavy filing to repair an unstable cut.

## What Chip Load Means at the Blade

Chip load can be estimated with a simple relationship:

**Chip load ≈ feed rate ÷ (RPM × number of teeth)**

Consider a 10-inch blade with 80 teeth running at 3,000 RPM. At a feed rate of 60 inches per minute, the nominal chip load is approximately 0.00025 inch per tooth. Doubling the feed to 120 inches per minute doubles the chip load to approximately 0.0005 inch per tooth, assuming the blade remains fully engaged and the profile does not move.

Those numbers are an illustration, not a universal feed-rate prescription. An extrusion is not a solid block. The blade may pass through a thin wall, an open cavity, a web, and another wall within the same revolution. The actual load changes as the tooth encounters each feature. That is why a blade and feed combination that works on solid bar can perform poorly on a thin-wall tube.

The formula still provides a useful way to reason about defects:

- Raising feed at the same RPM increases chip load.
- Raising tooth count at the same feed and RPM reduces chip load per tooth.
- Lowering RPM without reducing feed increases chip load.
- A loose or flexible profile changes chip load even when the saw settings remain unchanged.
- A dull or contaminated tooth behaves as if it has the wrong geometry, increasing rubbing and cutting force.

The ideal setting is not the slowest possible feed. Each tooth needs enough engagement to shear aluminum cleanly. A feed that is too timid can create more heat and burrs than a smooth, moderately firm feed.

## Blade Geometry Sets the Chip-Load Window

Blade selection determines how forgiving the cutting process will be. A blade intended for aluminum does more than survive the material; it shapes the chip so the tooth does not dig aggressively into a soft, ductile profile.

A triple-chip-grind blade alternates a trapezoid-shaped tooth with a flat clearing tooth. The first tooth begins the cut, and the following tooth clears the shoulders and corners. Sharing the work reduces the size of the bite at each tooth and usually improves the appearance of both faces of the cut.

A neutral or negative hook angle is equally important. Many wood blades use an aggressive positive hook that pulls the blade into the work. That self-feeding action is useful in some wood-cutting operations, but on aluminum it can make the profile climb, move, or shock the carbide tips. A negative hook makes the tooth engage more deliberately and helps keep the material pressed against the table and fence.

For many 10-inch miter saws, an 80- to 100-tooth carbide blade is a common starting range for aluminum extrusion. Twelve-inch saws often use blades in the 80- to 120-tooth range. Those figures are not rules. The wall thickness, profile height, blade diameter, gullet size, and saw speed all matter. The practical target is to keep at least two or three teeth engaged in the thinnest wall without packing the gullets with chips.

More teeth do not automatically produce a cleaner cut. If the blade has so many teeth that each one only rubs the aluminum, heat rises and chips can weld to the tooth faces. Thick solid sections may need fewer teeth and larger gullets so the blade can carry chips away. Thin walls generally need more teeth so a single tooth does not take an oversized bite.

An abrasive wheel creates a different problem. It removes aluminum through friction rather than controlled shearing, so the wheel loads with metal, heats the profile, and leaves a broad burr. A carbide-tipped blade rated for nonferrous metal gives the operator a controllable chip load instead.

## Feed Control Matters More Than the Phrase Slow and Steady

The usual advice to cut aluminum slowly is incomplete. The useful instruction is to feed at a steady rate that keeps the teeth cutting instead of rubbing, without forcing them into the profile.

Let the blade reach full speed before it contacts the aluminum. If the operator lowers the head while the motor is still accelerating, the feed movement can be large relative to the blade speed. The teeth take an oversized initial bite, which can cause grabbing, a chipped carbide tip, or movement at the fence.

During the cut, the sound and chip shape provide practical feedback:

- A falling motor pitch, strong vibration, large curled chips, or visible profile movement usually indicate excessive chip load or inadequate support.
- A high-pitched squeal, polished or smeared aluminum, welded chips, and a hot cut face usually indicate rubbing from insufficient effective chip load, a dull blade, or poor lubrication.
- Small, consistent chips, a stable motor note, and a cut face with uniform tooth marks indicate that the process is closer to its intended range.

Do not pause halfway through the cut. A stationary tooth rubbing one location generates heat and can leave a local gouge. Do not force the head through the final wall either. Maintain control through the exit, then keep the blade down until it stops completely. Lifting a still-spinning blade can catch the fresh edge or throw the offcut into the teeth.

On a fixed-speed saw, feed pressure is usually the main chip-load adjustment. Change it in small increments and observe the cut. Changing the blade, lubricant, clamping method, and feed all at once makes it impossible to identify which variable solved the problem.

## Support Keeps Chip Load From Changing Mid-Cut

Clamping is often described as a safety step, but it is also a cutting-accuracy step. If the profile flexes, twists, or lifts, the distance between the teeth and the material changes. The tooth then takes a different bite from one moment to the next, even though the operator's feed is constant.

Clamp T-slot profiles on solid faces or near their corners. A clamp pad placed directly on a thin slot lip can deform the lip and prevent slot hardware from sliding later. If the clamp must sit above a slot, use a filler strip, spare slot nut, or broad sacrificial pad to distribute the force.

Hollow profiles require support inside and outside. A snug wood or plastic insert near the cut can stop a thin tube wall from collapsing, while a V-block or shaped support keeps the outside from rocking. The insert should support the wall without distorting the profile and must remain clear of the blade path.

Support the stock on both sides of the blade. An unsupported offcut can drop as the cut opens, pulling the profile into the blade or tearing the final wall. A long piece can also sag on the infeed side, changing its relationship to the fence before the blade reaches the cut line.

A common failure pattern is a clean entry followed by a ragged exit. The blade is often blamed, but the offcut has usually lost support or the profile has lifted from the table. The final tooth engagement then becomes much more aggressive than the initial engagement. Adding support at the exit can improve the end without changing the blade at all.

## Why a Square End Is Also a Chip-Load Problem

A saw set to zero degrees does not guarantee a square end. The angle stop controls the blade, but it cannot stop the extrusion from rocking or the blade from deflecting under uneven cutting force.

When a T-slot profile is balanced on a thin edge, the first tooth can rotate it slightly. When the blade meets an internal web, resistance rises on one side of the profile and the part can shift again. The result is a cut face that is technically made at a 90-degree saw setting but is not square to the profile.

For a square cut, place one substantial face against the fence and another on the table. Use the profile's solid corners to carry clamp pressure. Verify the saw's zero-degree setting with a reliable square rather than trusting the printed scale. After the first cut, check the end on two adjacent faces. A square that only touches one face can miss a twist across the profile.

Miter cuts magnify the same problem. A small movement during one 45-degree cut creates a gap when the mating piece is installed. The longer diagonal contact of a miter also increases side loading, so shaped supports and a controlled feed become more important than simply slowing the saw down.

## Heat Changes the Effective Chip Load

Aluminum conducts heat well, but the small cutting zone can still become hot when teeth rub or chips remain trapped in the gullets. Hot aluminum tends to adhere to carbide and steel. The resulting built-up edge changes the tooth shape, reduces chip clearance, and increases cutting force.

A light application of cutting wax or lubricant formulated for aluminum can reduce friction and help chips release from the teeth. Use only enough to assist the cut, and keep residue off surfaces that must be bonded, painted, or assembled with friction-based hardware. A lubricant can improve a sharp blade's performance, but it cannot repair a chipped or dull tooth.

Blade cleanliness is part of chip-load control. Aluminum packed onto the tooth face effectively changes the rake angle and can make a previously reliable blade grab. If the cut becomes progressively rougher during a run, inspect the blade before increasing feed pressure. A dirty blade often causes the operator to push harder, which compounds the problem.

## Read the Cut End as a Process Diagnosis

The end face contains evidence about what happened between the first tooth and the last. Use the defect to identify the unstable part of the process rather than filing it away without investigation.

- **Heavy rolled burrs on several edges:** The chip load may be too high, the blade may have a positive hook, or the blade may be dull. Confirm the blade type and sharpness, then reduce feed pressure slightly.
- **Smeared aluminum or chips welded to the teeth:** The teeth are rubbing or running hot. Check blade condition, use suitable lubrication, and increase feed only enough to establish a real cutting action.
- **A burr concentrated on the final wall:** The offcut may be unsupported, the profile may be lifting, or the operator may be raising the blade before it stops.
- **A diagonal or tilted end:** Look for rocking against the fence, over-clamping on a slot lip, blade deflection, runout, or an inaccurate zero-degree stop.
- **Regular chatter marks across the face:** Reduce unsupported overhang, improve the fixture, and inspect the blade for runout or damaged teeth.
- **One face clean and the opposite face torn:** The profile may be rotating or the tooth may be exiting a thin wall without adequate backing.

Not every problem is chip load. A part can have a beautiful cut face and still be the wrong length because the kerf was placed on the finished side of the mark. Use a stop block for repeated work, account for kerf, and measure from a verified datum. Chip-load control determines the quality of the cut; it does not correct a bad layout.

## The Same Principle Changes With the Profile

A thin-wall tube needs many teeth in contact, internal support, and careful exit control. Too few teeth allow one tooth to take a large bite from a flexible wall. Too much clamp pressure can crush the tube before cutting begins.

A thick solid bar can tolerate a lower tooth count with larger gullets. That blade can carry more material away, but the operator still has to maintain enough feed to prevent rubbing. A very fine blade on a thick section may sound smooth while building heat because the teeth are not taking a meaningful chip.

A T-slot frame profile adds another concern: the slot lips are structurally weaker than the outer corners. The profile should be oriented so solid faces carry the clamp load, and the stop should contact a consistent reference face. For repeated frame parts, the first cut should be checked for length, square, and burr condition before the stop is used for the remaining pieces.

In all three cases, the material changes, but the rule does not. The blade must remove a predictable chip while the profile stays where the fixture placed it.

## A Repeatable Setup Built Around Chip Load

1. Identify the thinnest wall and the thickest section of the profile before choosing tooth count.
2. Use a sharp carbide blade rated for nonferrous metal, preferably with a triple-chip grind and neutral or negative hook.
3. Check the blade's diameter, arbor, kerf, and maximum RPM against the saw.
4. Seat the extrusion firmly against the fence and table, using broad pads on solid faces.
5. Add internal support for hollow sections and support the stock on both sides of the cut.
6. Apply a light, aluminum-compatible lubricant if the blade and machine allow it.
7. Start the saw, wait for full speed, and feed with continuous pressure rather than a slow pause-and-push motion.
8. Maintain support and control through the final wall, then wait for the blade to stop before raising it.
9. Inspect the first cut for burr shape, smearing, squareness, and tooth marks. Adjust one variable at a time.

The most expensive saw cannot compensate for a profile that moves, a blade that rubs, or a feed that changes unpredictably. The best aluminum extrusion cutting setup is the one that keeps chip load consistent from the first tooth at entry to the last tooth at exit. Once that condition is controlled, square ends and minimal burrs become repeatable results rather than occasional luck.
