# Aluminum Extrusion Sourcing: Validate Your First China Order

## The first order is a validation event, not a price test

A first order from a Chinese aluminum extrusion supplier should answer one operational question: can the supplier repeatedly produce the exact profile your product needs, document that compliance, protect it in transit, and deliver it at a predictable landed cost? Until that question is answered, a low per-kilogram quote is only a hypothesis.

Aluminum extrusion is often treated as a simple commodity purchase because the material is sold by weight. The finished product is not simple. Alloy, temper, die design, press capability, quenching, stretching, aging, surface treatment, cutting, inspection, and packaging all influence whether the profile will function in the finished assembly.

The first order therefore has a different purpose from a routine replenishment order. It is a controlled validation run. Its job is to turn supplier promises into measurable evidence before a buyer commits to production volume.

Before requesting prices, assemble a [first-order sourcing checklist](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-china-essential-points-before-your-first-order_n957) that converts every promise into a requirement someone can inspect, test, or document. The checklist should cover alloy and temper, drawing revision, critical tolerances, finish, inspection records, packaging, shipping terms, and change control.

## Why the lowest quote can be the expensive option

A per-kilogram quote is only one component of the purchase cost. A more useful calculation is:

> Landed cost = product price + tooling + finishing + machining + packaging + freight + duty and tax + inspection + rework + delay exposure.

Two suppliers can quote the same nominal alloy and profile while pricing very different products. One may assume an existing die, mill finish, broad tolerances, basic bundles, and no third-party inspection. Another may include a new die, anodizing, protective interleaving, material certificates, and documented dimensional checks.

Consider an illustrative order of 2,000 kilograms. Supplier A quotes $4.25 per kilogram, while Supplier B quotes $4.55. The apparent difference is $600. If Supplier A's lot produces an 8% assembly failure that requires replacement, 160 kilograms of material would need to be remade. At the quoted product rate, replacement material alone would cost $680, already exceeding the apparent saving before labor, freight, inspection, production downtime, or customer penalties are included.

That example is not a prediction of any supplier's defect rate. It demonstrates why the first comparison must be specification-to-specification, not price-to-price. A higher quote can be the lower-cost option if it purchases evidence, consistency, and usable material. A low quote can be attractive only because important obligations remain undefined.

Aluminum prices may also be tied to an index, a billet surcharge, or a limited quote-validity period. Ask whether the price is based on theoretical or actual weight, whether tooling is separate, what finishing is included, and which Incoterm applies. Otherwise, even a technically acceptable order can produce an unpleasant landed-cost surprise.

## The four proof points a first order must establish

### 1. Material identity and temper

An alloy number by itself is not a complete material specification. 6063 and 6061 are both common extrusion alloys, but they are not interchangeable. 6063 is frequently selected for smooth architectural surfaces and easier extrusion, while 6061 is often selected when strength and machinability carry greater weight.

Temper matters just as much. T5 and T6 describe different heat-treatment conditions and can produce materially different strength, hardness, residual stress, and machining behavior. A supplier that confirms only 6063, without confirming the temper, has not confirmed the material your design requires.

The purchase order and inspection plan should identify:

- Alloy and temper
- Applicable material standard
- Heat or lot identification
- Chemical composition record
- Tensile strength, yield strength, and elongation where required
- Material test certificate supplied with the shipment

Generic alloy data from a catalog is not a substitute for lot-specific documentation. The first order should establish that the supplier can connect the material certificate to the actual bundles delivered.

### 2. Cross-section accuracy and functional fit

A profile can meet its overall width and height while still failing in the assembly. A gasket channel that is a few tenths of a millimeter too narrow, a T-slot that does not accept the intended nut, or a mounting surface with excessive twist can stop production even when the profile looks acceptable.

Separate dimensions into three groups:

- **Critical-to-function dimensions:** slots, grooves, gasket channels, mating surfaces, hole locations, wall thicknesses, and machining datums
- **Controlled dimensions:** overall width and height, cut length, straightness, flatness, bow, and twist
- **Cosmetic dimensions:** visible surface uniformity, die lines, and appearance requirements

This classification prevents two common mistakes. The first is under-specifying the dimensions that make the part work. The second is demanding expensive precision on every surface when only a few features affect assembly.

A common extrusion tolerance may fall somewhere around ±0.1 to ±0.3 millimeters depending on the dimension, profile complexity, and applicable standard, but that range is not a universal promise. The drawing should identify the governing standard, datum system, measurement method, sampling plan, and acceptance limit.

The first order should also test long-length behavior. A short cut sample may hide bow, twist, or gradual dimensional drift along a six-meter profile. Measurements should be taken at multiple locations and, where practical, from production bundles made at the beginning, middle, and end of the run.

### 3. Finish performance in the real environment

A color match is not the same as a durable finish. Surface treatment must be selected against the installation environment and the way the part will be handled.

For example, anodizing creates an integral oxide layer and can provide strong wear and corrosion resistance. Standard architectural thicknesses may range from roughly 10 to 18 microns, while heavier exterior requirements may call for 18 microns or more under the applicable market standard. Powder coating offers broad color and texture choices, but its long-term UV behavior is different from premium PVDF coating. A coastal facade and an indoor machine guard should not receive the same finish specification by default.

The first order should define:

- Treatment type and applicable standard
- Minimum coating or anodizing thickness
- Color reference, such as an approved physical sample or RAL value
- Gloss, texture, and color-variation limits
- Adhesion and sealing requirements
- Which surfaces are exposed and which are hidden
- Inspection lighting and viewing distance for cosmetic approval

The finish also has to be tested in the assembly. Check whether protective film leaves residue, whether anodizing changes the fit of mating components, whether powder coating builds up in channels, and whether cut ends require additional protection. A finish that looks excellent on a loose sample can still interfere with hardware installation.

### 4. Delivery condition and packaging

The manufacturing process does not end when the extrusion leaves the press. Long, thin profiles are vulnerable to scratches, crushed ends, bending, moisture, and movement inside a shipping container.

Packaging requirements should specify interleaving material, protective film, bundle restraint, end protection, pallet or crate construction, moisture control, bundle identification, and maximum bundle weight. The receiving team should be able to match each bundle to the material certificate and inspection report.

A profile that passed the factory's final inspection but arrived bent or scratched is still a failed order from the user's perspective. Packaging is therefore part of product quality, not an administrative detail added after the technical specification is complete.

## Write the RFQ so acceptance is possible

Many disputes begin with adjectives such as high quality, strong, premium finish, or export grade. Those terms may sound clear to a buyer, but they do not establish an acceptance test. A supplier cannot reliably quote or manufacture against an undefined adjective.

A useful RFQ should contain the following information:

- **Application and loads:** Explain whether the profile is structural, decorative, thermal, electrical, or part of a moving assembly. Include span, load, temperature, corrosion exposure, and mating components where relevant.
- **Drawing controls:** Provide a dimensioned drawing, CAD file when useful, drawing revision, critical dimensions, datums, surface zones, and allowable deviations.
- **Material:** State alloy, temper, standard, expected mechanical properties, and whether substitutions require written approval.
- **Finish:** Identify treatment, thickness, color, gloss, texture, exposed surfaces, and environmental requirements.
- **Inspection:** Define dimensional sampling, mechanical testing, coating checks, visual acceptance, certificates, and whether third-party inspection is required.
- **Commercial basis:** Separate die cost, extrusion cost, finish, machining, packaging, freight, and taxes. State whether pricing is per kilogram, piece, meter, or a combination.
- **Delivery:** Specify cut lengths, quantity tolerance, minimum order quantity, target date, Incoterm, and packaging method.

An illustrative line item might read as follows:

> 6063-T5; drawing Rev C; slot width 8.00 ±0.10 mm; cut length 6,000 ±2 mm; anodized 18 μm minimum on exposed surfaces; no visible scratches at 1 meter under agreed lighting; material and coating reports supplied with each lot; interleaved and wrapped bundles.

Those numbers are examples, not universal recommendations. Engineering calculations, the applicable standard, and the product's environment must set the final values. The important point is that the supplier can now quote against the same measurable requirements the buyer will use for acceptance.

Require the supplier to list every exception in the quotation. If the proposed die cannot hold a feature, if a finish is unavailable in the requested color, or if the supplier needs a different temper, that deviation should appear before the order is placed. Silent substitutions make supplier comparison meaningless.

## Validate capability in stages

### Screen capability before commissioning tooling

A website, catalog, or quality certificate does not prove that a factory can produce a particular profile. Before paying for a custom die, confirm that the supplier has the press range, die-making capability, finishing equipment, test equipment, and production experience required by the design.

Useful evidence includes:

- Examples of comparable profiles and finishes
- Press size and maximum profile envelope
- In-house or qualified die-shop capability
- Mechanical and dimensional testing equipment
- Coating or anodizing inspection records
- Relevant certifications such as ISO 9001, IATF 16949, or AS9100 when the application requires them
- A documented method for handling nonconforming material

Certification is a useful filter, not a verdict. ISO 9001 may show that a quality system exists, but it does not prove that a specific 6063 profile will meet a tight slot tolerance or arrive without surface damage.

### Treat die approval as process approval

A custom die is not merely a shape-making expense. It determines how metal flows, how walls balance, where die lines appear, and how much correction may be needed during trial extrusion. Thin walls, deep hollows, sharp internal corners, and large differences in wall thickness deserve an engineering review before the die is released.

Custom die development often takes roughly two to four weeks, with trial extrusion requiring another one to two weeks, although complexity and factory loading can extend those ranges. Put die ownership, correction responsibility, trial limits, and approval rights in writing before production begins.

A good die approval process evaluates more than the outline of the cross-section. It checks critical dimensions, wall thickness, surface zones, straightness, cut behavior, assembly fit, and the supplier's ability to reproduce the result after the die is corrected.

### Use a pilot lot to measure variation

A hand-selected sample answers only whether one piece can look good. It does not establish process stability. A pilot lot should be large enough to reveal variation across the run and should be sampled from different production points.

For a pilot, inspect:

- Critical dimensions at several points along each selected length
- Straightness, flatness, bow, and twist
- Wall thickness and hollow sections
- Hardness or mechanical properties when required
- Finish thickness, color, gloss, and adhesion
- Cut length, burrs, and end condition
- Assembly with the actual gasket, bracket, fastener, lens, or mating part

If the die has multiple cavities, include samples from each cavity. If the profile is later machined, test the machining operation as part of the validation rather than assuming a dimensional issue can be corrected later.

### Inspect before and after shipping

Pre-shipment inspection catches problems while the supplier still has access to the equipment and remaining material. Incoming inspection confirms that the shipment survived handling and transportation.

The two inspections should use the same drawing revision and acceptance criteria. If the pre-shipment report says the material passed but the receiving team finds damaged bundles, missing certificates, or mixed lots, the order has not met its practical requirement.

## Supplier behavior is technical evidence

The most revealing supplier response is often not the first price. It is the response to an awkward engineering question.

A capable supplier is likely to ask about load, span, environment, assembly sequence, annual volume, visible surfaces, and the difference between critical and noncritical dimensions. The supplier may recommend a larger corner radius, a more balanced wall design, a different temper, or a change that improves metal flow. Those questions indicate an attempt to control the process rather than merely win the order.

Warning signs include:

- Quoting from a photograph without confirming the drawing
- Promising every tolerance without reviewing the cross-section
- Treating 6063 and 6061 as interchangeable
- Refusing to state finish thickness or inspection method
- Providing only a hand-picked sample with no pilot-lot records
- Offering a production date that ignores die development and trial approval
- Giving no specific packaging plan for long profiles
- Changing material, finish, or shipping terms after approval

These are not just communication problems. They are leading indicators of production risk.

One practical first-order supplier score can assign 30% to technical response and design review, 25% to sample or pilot results, 20% to quality records and testing, 15% to capacity and logistics, and 10% to price. The weights can change by application, but the structure prevents a small price difference from overpowering clear technical evidence.

## The validation target changes with the application

The same validation principle applies to different products, but the proof required is not identical.

### Architectural window or door profile

For a window or door system, the first order should prioritize 6063 alloy and the selected temper, surface appearance, gasket channels, hardware interfaces, straightness, and finish consistency. The profile should be assembled with the actual gasket, corner hardware, glass interface, or thermal-break component.

A profile can have an attractive anodized or powder-coated surface and still fail because a channel is too tight, a wall is distorted, or a mating face is not straight enough for the assembly process. In this case, cosmetic approval without fit testing provides false confidence.

### Industrial frame or T-slot profile

For a machine frame, the priorities may shift toward slot width, base flatness, wall thickness, straightness, twist, machining datums, and the mechanical properties of the selected temper. Test the real nuts, brackets, linear guides, tapped holes, and fasteners. Surface appearance may be secondary unless the equipment is customer-facing.

A visually perfect profile with a distorted slot can be unusable. A profile with minor die lines may be fully acceptable if it holds the required load and assembles accurately. The first order must prove the features that make the specific product work, not a generic idea of good aluminum.

## Convert the first-order results into a repeatable standard

Validation has little value if the learning disappears when the next purchase order is issued. Before releasing production volume, preserve the evidence in a controlled baseline:

- Approved drawing revision and CAD files
- Retained physical sample or golden sample
- Recorded measurements from the pilot lot
- Material and finish certificates
- Approved packaging photographs or packaging specification
- List of open deviations and corrective actions
- Agreed inspection frequency and sampling plan
- Confirmed die ownership and correction terms
- Commercial terms that identify what is included in the price
- Change-notification requirement for materials, tooling, processing, finishing, or packaging

The change-control requirement is especially important. A billet-source change, die correction, press change, different quench or aging practice, new finishing line, or revised packaging method can alter the result. The supplier should notify the buyer before making a change that could affect form, fit, function, appearance, or delivery condition.

Compare the second order against the first using the same critical measurements. Pass or fail is useful, but trend data is better. A dimension that remains technically acceptable while drifting toward the limit may reveal die wear, changing press settings, or an unstable finishing process.

## Ask for proof before asking for volume

The strongest sourcing question is not What is your price per kilogram? It is Can you prove that this exact specification will be produced repeatedly and arrive usable?

That question changes the entire first-order process. The drawing becomes an acceptance document. The sample becomes a process test. The inspection report becomes evidence rather than paperwork. Packaging becomes part of quality, and the quote becomes comparable only when its assumptions are visible.

Price still matters, but it should influence the decision after material identity, functional fit, finish performance, repeatability, and delivery condition have been validated. Volume should be the consequence of that proof, not a substitute for it.
