# Aluminum Extrusion HS Code: Classify the Shape, Not the Sales Name

## The decisive insight: customs classifies the thing, not the name

The most expensive classification errors begin with a product name that sounds precise but is legally broad. Aluminum extrusion describes a manufacturing process. It does not tell customs whether the imported item is a bar, profile, tube, pipe, structural part, or finished article.

Window profile, solar profile, heat sink, and frame member are commercial labels. Tariff schedules classify physical merchandise under legal descriptions. A 6063 billet pushed through a die can emerge as an open angle, a closed architectural section, a circular tube, or a finned heat sink. All may be sold as aluminum extrusions, but their customs treatment may differ.

The practical principle behind any [aluminum HS code guide](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-hs-code-decoded-stop-overpaying-customs-duties_n965) is the same: customs classifies merchandise in the condition imported, applying heading text, legal notes, and national tariff rules. The code is a conclusion drawn from evidence, not a label chosen because it produces a convenient duty rate.

> A defensible classification can be reconstructed from the drawing, material certificate, and processing record.

That principle changes the entire workflow. Instead of searching a tariff database for a phrase that resembles the invoice, the importer first establishes what the product physically is. The tariff code then becomes the result of that analysis.

## Why aluminum extrusion is an incomplete customs description

An invoice that says aluminum extrusion leaves several decisive questions unanswered:

* What is the alloy composition, and is the metal alloyed or not alloyed?
* Is the item a bar, rod, profile, tube, or pipe?
* Does the cross-section contain an enclosed void, an open slot, or only decorative recesses?
* Is the product still stock profile, or has it become a fabricated component?
* Which country-specific tariff extension applies to the six-digit HS foundation?

The intended use can support a classification, particularly when distinguishing a finished article from stock material, but use alone rarely overrides objective characteristics. A profile sold for solar panels does not automatically receive a solar-specific code. A profile marketed for windows does not automatically become a window structure. Customs begins with what arrived at the border.

The six-digit HS code provides the international foundation. The importing country then adds national digits, statistical suffixes, and sometimes special provisions. A supplier may correctly identify a six-digit code for export purposes while the importer still needs to select a different national tariff line, duty treatment, or trade-remedy provision at destination.

## The physical chain that produces the code

### 1. Start with alloy composition, not the alloy nickname

Alloy designations such as 6061, 6063, 6005, and 7075 are useful starting points, but they are not a substitute for chemical evidence. A mill test certificate or material certificate should identify the elemental composition and the temper, such as T5 or T6.

The temper describes heat treatment and mechanical condition. It does not replace the composition analysis needed to distinguish non-alloyed aluminum from aluminum alloys or to determine whether a national tariff schedule separates alloy families. Two products can share a similar appearance and temper while falling into different classification branches because their chemistry differs.

For recurring imports, the material certificate should be tied to the exact product SKU or billet batch. A general supplier statement that the factory uses 6063 aluminum is weaker evidence than a certificate showing the composition of the material used for the shipment.

### 2. Identify the product form

Heading 7604 is the usual starting point for aluminum bars, rods, and profiles. At the six-digit level, common branches include:

* **7604.10** for aluminum that is not alloyed
* **7604.21** for hollow profiles of aluminum alloys
* **7604.29** for other profiles of aluminum alloys

Those branches are not a shortcut. The merchandise must first meet the legal description of the relevant form. A complex extruded shape with a uniform cross-section may be a profile, while a simple solid circular product may be a bar or rod.

A product with a void also requires a careful check. Heading 7608 covers aluminum tubes and pipes, including alloyed aluminum tubes and pipes under the relevant subheading. Therefore, an item should not be placed under 7604.21 merely because it has an empty space. The analysis must determine whether the product is legally a hollow profile or a tube or pipe under the applicable tariff text and notes.

This is where many supplier catalogs oversimplify the issue. Extrusion is the production method; profile, tube, and pipe are tariff concepts.

### 3. Treat the cross-section as primary evidence

The most reliable classification document is often a technical drawing showing the cross-section perpendicular to the product length. A product photograph can hide internal cavities, openings, wall thickness, and the difference between an open channel and an enclosed void.

An open groove or T-slot does not automatically make a profile hollow. A decorative recess does not have the same significance as a continuous enclosed cavity. Conversely, a profile with several internal channels may require a more detailed review than a simple solid shape.

The drawing should show:

* The complete perimeter of the section
* Internal cavities and openings
* Wall thicknesses
* Major dimensions
* Whether the section is uniform along its length
* Any features added after extrusion

A customs reviewer should be able to look at the drawing and understand why the product was treated as a profile rather than a tube, pipe, bar, or finished article.

### 4. Examine the condition imported

Classification follows the merchandise as imported, not necessarily the form in which it left the extrusion press. Surface finishing such as anodizing, powder coating, polishing, or wood-grain treatment often preserves the basic identity of an aluminum profile, but it should still be documented.

More substantial work can change the analysis. Cutting to length alone may leave the product as profile stock. Drilling, notching, milling, adding brackets, fitting hardware, or assembling multiple pieces can move the merchandise toward a more specific heading for structures, parts, or other articles. The result depends on the degree of manufacture and the legal terms of the destination tariff.

The key question is not whether a machine touched the product. It is whether the imported object remains primarily an aluminum profile or has become a specifically identifiable manufactured article.

## Three situations where the sales label fails

### A 6063 product family with three different physical identities

Imagine an invoice listing three SKUs as 6063 window extrusion:

* A solid 6063-T5 L-angle shipped in six-meter lengths is generally a candidate for the other alloy profile branch under heading 7604 because it has no enclosed void.
* A rectangular 6063-T5 architectural section with a continuous enclosed cavity may be a candidate for the hollow alloy profile branch under 7604.21, provided it is legally a profile rather than a tube or pipe.
* A round 6061-T6 tube is normally analyzed under the aluminum tube and pipe heading rather than forced into the profile heading simply because it was made by extrusion.

All three products may be anodized, packed together, and sold for window manufacturing. Their common end use does not make them one tariff product. The cross-section and imported condition remain decisive.

### A finned heat sink before and after fabrication

A long, uniform 6063 heat-sink extrusion with fins formed as part of the die is generally analyzed as an aluminum profile if it remains stock extrusion. Cutting it into standard lengths may not change that identity because the cross-sectional form is still the same.

Now consider a different shipment containing a machined base, drilled mounting holes, attached brackets, thermal pads, and an installed fan. The product may no longer be ordinary profile stock. Its function and degree of manufacture can require analysis under a heading for a specific part or article.

The product name heat sink does not settle either result. The manufacturing record and the condition in the shipping package do.

### A curtain-wall member shipped in two different conditions

A straight, anodized mullion shipped in long lengths for fabrication at the destination is materially different from a pre-cut, drilled, notched, and packaged member designed for a particular building opening. Both may be called curtain-wall profiles by the seller.

The first shipment is easier to support as profile stock. The second requires review of whether the processing has produced a structural part or another more specific article. Cutting and drilling do not automatically determine a new code, but they are facts that must be evaluated rather than hidden inside a generic description.

## The duty impact makes the distinction commercially important

Classification is not an academic exercise. Assume, solely for illustration, that two legally distinct tariff lines carry general duty rates of 1.5% and 5% in the destination country. On a customs value of $80,000:

* At 1.5%, the duty would be $1,200.
* At 5%, the duty would be $4,000.
* The difference would be $2,800 on one shipment.

Ten comparable shipments would create a $28,000 difference before brokerage charges, storage, inspection costs, or any additional trade measures. Actual rates vary by country, tariff year, origin, and product scope, so the calculation is an illustration rather than a universal rate chart.

The lower rate is not the correct target. Selecting a low-duty line without physical support can lead to reassessment, interest, penalties, shipment delays, and a much larger exposure if an anti-dumping, countervailing, safeguard, or other special measure applies. Correct classification protects the importer because it makes the duty outcome traceable to the merchandise.

## Build an evidence file around the physical product

A reliable classification file should contain more than a supplier invoice. It should allow an independent reviewer to recreate the reasoning months after the shipment clears.

Include the following:

1. **A product identity statement** describing alloy, temper, form, dimensions, finish, and imported condition.
2. **The material certificate** showing chemical composition and connecting the material to the shipment or SKU.
3. **A cross-sectional drawing** showing cavities, openings, wall thickness, and uniformity.
4. **The manufacturing route** identifying extrusion, cutting, drilling, machining, assembly, and finishing operations.
5. **Photographs or packaging records** showing how the goods were presented at import.
6. **A tariff rationale** explaining why the selected heading is preferable to nearby alternatives such as tubes, pipes, structures, or other articles.
7. **The destination tariff line and effective date** because national extensions and duty measures can change even when the six-digit HS foundation remains stable.

Compare the difference between these descriptions:

*Weak:* aluminum profiles, 5,000 kilograms.

*Defensible:* 6063-T5 aluminum alloy, solid L-angle profile, uniform 6,000-millimeter lengths, anodized finish, no holes or attached hardware, imported as extruded profile stock.

The stronger description does not manipulate the tariff result. It gives customs the facts required to test the classification. Precision must be supported by drawings and certificates; invented precision is no better than a vague invoice.

Classification evidence should also be separated from origin evidence. A mill certificate may establish chemistry, but it does not by itself prove where the aluminum was smelted, cast, extruded, or substantially transformed. Classification, origin, valuation, and trade-remedy scope are related compliance questions, not interchangeable ones.

## Make classification part of engineering change control

For companies importing the same product repeatedly, the strongest control is to connect the tariff decision to the engineering record. A code should belong to a defined SKU and drawing revision, not to an entire product family merely because every item contains aluminum or uses the 6063 alloy.

Trigger a classification review when:

* A die change adds or removes an enclosed cavity.
* An open channel becomes a closed section.
* The alloy or temper changes.
* A round product changes from bar to tube, or vice versa.
* The supplier adds drilling, milling, notching, brackets, or assembly.
* Long stock changes to pre-fabricated kits.
* The product begins shipping to a new destination country.
* A new trade measure or tariff revision affects the relevant heading.

This approach catches classification changes before the container is loaded. It also prevents a broker from receiving a new product under an old code simply because the sales department kept the same family name.

## When a formal ruling is the right tool

Some boundaries deserve more than an internal opinion, especially when the same product is imported in large volumes or when the alternatives have materially different duty consequences. Common escalation points include:

* Hollow profile versus tube or pipe
* Profile stock versus a finished structural part
* Bar or rod versus profile
* A product near an alloy-composition boundary
* Fabricated goods potentially covered by a trade-remedy order

A broker can compare the product with prior customs rulings and prepare a classification memo. For a recurring or high-value import, a binding ruling from the relevant customs authority can provide stronger certainty. The submission should include the actual drawing, composition, photographs, processing details, and a precise question. A ruling based on a different cross-section or imported condition may not apply to a redesigned product.

The practical rule is simple: begin with the cross-section drawing, material certificate, and condition imported. Do not begin with the duty rate or the supplier's sales name. If the code can be traced back to the physical evidence, the aluminum extrusion HS code is a defensible compliance decision rather than a guess copied from an invoice.
