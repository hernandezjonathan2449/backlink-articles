# Aluminum Extrusion Die Ownership: The Terms That Protect Supply

## The tooling charge is not the ownership agreement

A custom aluminum profile is often treated as the product, while the extrusion die is treated as a setup expense. That view creates a serious supply risk. The profile cannot be reproduced economically without a suitable die, and the supplier holding that die can control how quickly the buyer changes manufacturers.

An invoice line labeled tooling charge does not answer the central question: who controls the die when the commercial relationship changes? The charge may cover a die that belongs to the buyer, a die that remains the supplier’s property, or a tool whose cost is gradually recovered through the piece price. Those arrangements can produce similar quotes while giving the buyer very different rights.

A written [die ownership checklist](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/how-to-choose-an-aluminum-extrusion-manufacturer-and-own-your-dies_n1032) belongs in the RFQ and supply agreement before steel is cut. The agreement should treat ownership as a group of separate rights rather than a single sentence.

## Ownership has five separate dimensions

### 1. Legal title

Title answers who owns the physical die. The contract should identify each die by number, profile revision, and related purchase order. It should also state when title transfers. Possible triggers include payment of the tooling charge, payment of a defined portion of the first order, or completion of an agreed production volume.

The transfer event matters because a supplier may design and pay for the die before receiving the buyer’s payment. If the contract never states when title passes, both parties may interpret the same invoice differently.

Replacement dies require the same treatment. A worn die may be replaced several times during a long program, especially when the profile has thin walls, complex hollow sections, or demanding cosmetic requirements. If the original die belongs to the buyer but the replacement remains the supplier’s property, ownership has not been fully protected.

### 2. Custody and control

A buyer can own a die while the supplier stores it for years. That arrangement is normal, but custody must be defined. The supplier should agree to:

- Tag and identify the die as the buyer’s property.
- Keep it separate from unassigned or supplier-owned tooling.
- Protect it from loss, corrosion, misuse, and unauthorized modification.
- Disclose any location where a subcontractor stores or services it.
- Maintain appropriate insurance for theft, fire, and physical damage.
- Allow reasonable inspection or photographic confirmation of its condition.

The agreement should also prevent the supplier from using the die as leverage in an unrelated payment dispute. A disagreement over a production invoice should not automatically allow the supplier to hold customer-owned tooling that has already been paid for. The release clause should identify exactly which tooling-related amounts must be settled before shipment.

### 3. Exclusive use

A die built for a buyer’s profile should not be used to produce that profile for another customer. The restriction should cover the die, profile drawings, and any confidential design information supplied with the project.

This matters even when the profile appears simple. A supplier may argue that the cross-section is generic or that it made minor changes to the die. A contract should distinguish between a buyer’s proprietary profile and ordinary industry knowledge. It should prohibit production for third parties when the die or design is specific to the buyer, while allowing the supplier to continue using its general extrusion expertise.

The same principle applies to samples. A supplier should not send the buyer’s profile to another customer as a design example without written permission. Cosmetic features, mounting interfaces, and internal channels can reveal more about a product than a basic confidentiality clause appears to cover.

### 4. Portability

Portability is the right most often confused with ownership. A die may legally belong to the buyer but still be difficult or impossible to run at another plant without modification.

Extrusion dies are designed around a press, container, die stack, support tooling, alloy, and profile geometry. A solid die for a small profile has different requirements from a porthole die for a hollow section. The die must work with the press container and with the backer, bolster, die ring, and other support components. A die stack built around one container size cannot automatically be installed in a different press.

Press tonnage alone is not enough. A second supplier may have sufficient force but lack the appropriate container diameter, die opening, runout equipment, or experience with the required hollow geometry. The die may need a new backer, a modified ring, or a complete redesign before the alternate plant can run it.

Portability therefore has two parts:

- **Physical portability:** the die and support tooling can be shipped and mounted at another plant.
- **Technical portability:** the alternate supplier has enough drawings, setup information, and process history to qualify the profile without starting from zero.

Before approving a supplier, ask a second qualified extruder to review the profile and confirm whether it could run the proposed die. That review should identify the compatible press range, container requirements, support tooling, and any likely modifications. A written answer is more useful than a general statement that the plant can make similar profiles.

### 5. Information and process records

The profile drawing is not the same as the die drawing. The buyer may own the cross-section while the die maker retains proprietary information about port layout, bridges, bearing lengths, welding chambers, and correction history.

That distinction deserves a deliberate contract decision. The buyer may need the complete die design in native CAD, a neutral-format drawing, or a defined set of manufacturing and setup records. The supplier may resist transferring its proprietary die design, but the buyer still needs enough information to preserve supply if the tool must move.

Useful records include:

- Die identification number and profile revision.
- Die material, heat treatment, and nitriding history.
- Die drawings or agreed transfer files.
- Press and container compatibility.
- Bearing lengths and critical die features.
- Trial reports and dimensional results.
- Correction history and the reason for each correction.
- Recommended setup and extrusion parameters.
- Photos showing the die condition before storage or shipment.

If the supplier will not transfer proprietary CAD, the agreement can require an escrow arrangement, a neutral-format release package, or access to the necessary records after a defined termination event. The goal is not to obtain every piece of the supplier’s intellectual property. The goal is to prevent a customer-owned tool from becoming unusable because its operating history is inaccessible.

## Why a paid-for die can still leave the buyer exposed

Consider a manufacturer buying a hollow 6063 profile for an equipment enclosure. The die charge is $4,000, and the annual demand is 60,000 pounds. Spread over the expected volume, the tooling burden is about $0.067 per pound. If the program ends after only 5,000 pounds, the effective tooling burden rises to $0.80 per pound.

That economics explains why some suppliers recover tooling through a higher piece price instead of a separate charge. A supplier may offer no upfront tooling fee but add $0.15 per pound to the conversion price. At 25,000 pounds per year, that premium recovers $3,750 annually. After two years, the buyer may have paid $7,500 without acquiring title to the die.

Neither arrangement is automatically wrong. The problem is an undisclosed arrangement. Every quote should answer four separate questions:

1. Is there a separate die charge?
2. Is any portion of the piece price recovering tooling?
3. When does title transfer?
4. What happens to the die if production stops before the expected volume is reached?

A supplier-financed die can be commercially attractive for a prototype or uncertain program. The buyer might accept supplier ownership in exchange for a lower initial investment, provided the quote clearly states the consequences. For a high-volume or strategically important profile, buyer ownership usually provides more valuable supply options.

## The tooling schedule should address the entire die life cycle

A short statement such as customer-owned tooling is not enough. A strong tooling schedule follows the die from design through retirement.

### Design and first trial

State who approves the die design and who pays for corrections when the first trial misses the drawing. A failed first trial can result from an unsuitable design, a manufacturing error, an unrealistic profile specification, or an incorrect process assumption. The contract should assign responsibility instead of leaving the parties to negotiate after the die has already been made.

The supplier should also identify how many trial and correction cycles are included in the tooling charge. A low die price may exclude the corrections needed to produce an acceptable profile.

### Normal wear and maintenance

Extrusion dies are consumable production tools. Hot aluminum, friction, thermal cycling, cleaning, correction, and nitriding gradually affect the die. Service life depends on alloy, profile geometry, bearing design, extrusion conditions, and the supplier’s maintenance practices.

The agreement should distinguish among:

- Routine cleaning and inspection.
- Normal re-nitriding or surface treatment.
- Corrections required to maintain the approved profile.
- Damage caused by improper handling or operation.
- Replacement required because of normal wear.
- Replacement required because of supplier negligence.

For example, the buyer may agree to pay for normal wear while requiring the supplier to pay for damage caused by improper storage or an avoidable press incident. The agreement should state that any replacement die made for the buyer’s profile receives the same ownership and release treatment as the original.

### Storage and inactivity

A supplier may decide that an inactive die takes up valuable storage space. Without a notice requirement, the tool could be scrapped before the buyer realizes that production has stopped long enough to trigger the supplier’s policy.

Require written notice before a die is retired or destroyed. The notice should identify the die, its condition, the proposed disposal date, and the buyer’s options for collection or shipment. A defined response period gives the buyer time to make a commercial decision instead of forcing an emergency response.

### Release after termination

The release clause should cover more than the physical die. It should specify:

- The number of business days allowed for release.
- The packaging method for protecting die surfaces.
- The party responsible for freight, insurance, and export documentation.
- The records and drawings that accompany the tool.
- The condition report required before shipment.
- The process for releasing replacement dies and support tooling.

Release should not depend on settling every unrelated invoice unless the parties deliberately agree to that remedy and local law permits it. A supplier’s broad right to retain tooling can turn a routine commercial dispute into a production shutdown.

## A die transfer is a qualification project, not a shipping event

When a buyer changes manufacturers, placing a die on a truck is only the first step. The alternate supplier must verify the tool, install it, run samples, inspect the profile, and confirm that downstream operations still work.

A practical transfer plan includes these steps:

1. **Confirm the alternate press before requesting the die.** Compare the die dimensions, container, support tooling, profile envelope, alloy, and required force with the destination plant.
2. **Create a condition record.** Photograph the die, record its identification number, and document visible wear, damage, and recent maintenance.
3. **Collect the technical package.** Include the profile drawing, die records, correction history, inspection data, and any agreed setup information.
4. **Define the first-article requirements.** State which dimensions, weld seams, surfaces, and mechanical properties must be checked after transfer.
5. **Protect revision control.** Confirm that the die and the profile drawing refer to the same revision. A transferred tool with an outdated profile is a common source of confusion.
6. **Plan temporary inventory.** The alternate plant may need several trials before it can support normal production. Finished-goods coverage can prevent the transfer from becoming an immediate customer-service problem.

For a critical profile, the buyer can perform this review before the original supplier has a problem. Ask the second supplier to evaluate the drawing and likely press fit during the sourcing process. That creates an exit plan while the relationship is still cooperative.

## A practical stress test for supplier selection

Imagine a window hardware manufacturer with a $6,000 custom die and a two-year supply history. An anodizing defect begins appearing on a visible face. The original supplier and finisher disagree about where the damage occurred. The buyer wants to move production.

If the buyer has only paid a tooling charge, the next questions may be difficult to answer. Is the die legally owned by the buyer? Is the supplier allowed to retain it until all invoices are paid? Can the die fit the alternate supplier’s press? Are the die drawings available? Who pays for a replacement backer or a correction after transfer?

If the tooling agreement answers those questions, the dispute remains a quality problem rather than becoming a tooling crisis. The buyer can request the die, provide the technical package to the alternate supplier, and begin qualification. If the agreement does not answer them, the buyer may need a new die while the original tool sits in storage, creating both cost and delay.

A useful pre-award test is to ask each supplier to respond to this scenario: production ends next month because of a quality, capacity, ownership, or business-continuity issue. Ask what the buyer receives, how quickly it receives it, and what charges could block release. The clarity of the answer often reveals more than a low tooling price.

## Red flags that indicate weak tooling control

Treat these responses as material risks:

- The supplier says the die is yours but will not state when title transfers.
- The quote includes a tooling charge but no die number or ownership language.
- The supplier cannot explain whether replacement dies belong to the buyer.
- The die may be scrapped after inactivity without written notice.
- Release is conditioned on payment of every open account balance.
- The supplier will not identify where the die is stored or whether a subcontractor holds it.
- No alternate supplier has reviewed press and container compatibility.
- The supplier refuses to provide any die history, inspection record, or setup information.
- A no-charge die is presented as free without explaining the piece-price recovery or volume commitment.
- The supplier claims portability but cannot identify the support tooling required at another plant.

> A die is not truly yours until you can identify it, control its use, obtain the information needed to run it, and place it into a qualified alternate press.

## The ownership decision belongs in the first quote comparison

Die ownership should be evaluated alongside alloy, capacity, finish, lead time, and price, not added after the supplier has been selected. The buyer’s real objective is not merely to possess a piece of hardened steel. It is to preserve the ability to keep making the profile when demand changes, quality fails, a plant becomes overloaded, or the relationship ends.

Before approving a custom extrusion supplier, require written answers to these questions:

- Who owns the original and all replacement dies?
- When does title pass?
- Where will each die be stored, and who bears the risk of loss?
- Can the supplier use the die or profile for anyone else?
- What maintenance and replacement costs belong to each party?
- What drawings, records, and setup information are transferable?
- Can another supplier run the die with its existing press and support tooling?
- How quickly will the die be released after termination?
- What notice is required before the die is modified, retired, or scrapped?

Those answers turn a tooling charge into a controlled supply asset. Without them, the buyer may have paid for the die while leaving the most important rights in the supplier’s hands.
