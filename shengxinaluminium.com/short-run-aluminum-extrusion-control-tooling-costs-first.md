# Short Run Aluminum Extrusion: Control Tooling Costs First

## The Fixed-Cost Reality of Short Run Aluminum Extrusion

For a 25- to 500-piece order, the aluminum itself is often not the main cost problem. The expensive part is paying for a die, press setup, trial run, first-article inspection, and finishing setup across a small number of parts.

A 50-piece order and a 500-piece order may use different quantities of metal, but they can require nearly the same die and engineering work. That makes short run extrusion a fixed-cost allocation problem before it becomes a material-pricing problem.

> The critical question is not how cheaply the aluminum can be purchased. It is how few times the project must pay for tooling and setup, and how many acceptable parts those fixed costs can produce.

The most effective [short-run tooling methods](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/short-run-aluminum-extrusion-techniques-that-slash-your-tooling-costs_n999) follow that principle. They reduce the initial die investment, reuse existing tooling where possible, simplify the profile, and spread unavoidable setup costs over a credible production quantity.

## Separate fixed costs from variable costs

A supplier quote becomes much easier to evaluate when it is divided into two categories:

`Total run cost = fixed costs + (variable cost per part × quantity)`

`Per-part cost = variable cost per part + (fixed costs ÷ quantity)`

Fixed costs commonly include:

- New die fabrication or modification
- Die design and engineering review
- Press setup, warm-up, and trial billets
- First-article inspection and dimensional approval
- Correction work after the initial trial
- Minimum charges for cutting, anodizing, powder coating, or packing

Variable costs usually include:

- Aluminum consumed by each part
- Extrusion and cutting time
- Per-piece finishing charges
- Secondary machining or drilling
- Packaging and unit-level handling

Consider an illustrative 100-piece project with these costs:

- Aluminum, extrusion, cutting, finishing, and packing: $18.50 per part
- New die: $1,800
- Setup and trial: $450
- First-article measurement: $250

The fixed cost is $2,500. At 100 pieces, that adds $25 per part, producing an estimated total of $43.50 per part. At 500 pieces, the same fixed cost adds only $5 per part, reducing the estimated total to $23.50 per part.

The raw aluminum portion might be $7.50 of the $18.50 variable cost. A 10% reduction in aluminum pricing would save only $0.75 per part. Reducing the die investment by $1,000 would save $10 per part on the 100-piece run. That is why a profile change or tooling modification can have a larger financial effect than a favorable metal-market quote.

## Die cost follows cross-section complexity, not piece count

An extrusion die is shaped by the profile cross-section. The die does not become simpler because the order contains fewer parts. A short order still needs the same openings, bridges, bearing surfaces, and flow-balancing features required by the geometry.

A simple open channel, angle, or flat bar can often use a relatively straightforward solid die. An enclosed tube or semi-hollow section needs a mandrel and bridge system. Thin unequal walls, deep narrow slots, sharp internal corners, and multiple cavities make metal flow harder to balance and increase the chance of trial corrections.

The cost difference can be substantial:

- An open profile may require limited machining and a short trial cycle.
- A hollow profile may require a more complex die assembly, additional adjustment, and more trial material.
- A multi-cavity profile can lower the unit cost at high volume but create excessive tooling cost for a small order.

For a short run, the least expensive design is not necessarily the one that uses the least aluminum. It is the one that meets the functional requirement with the least expensive and most predictable die.

### Design changes with unusually high financial leverage

Several design decisions directly affect the fixed-cost burden:

- Use a standard circumscribing circle and common die-holder size whenever the application allows.
- Keep wall thickness as uniform as practical so the die does not need aggressive flow balancing.
- Remove decorative ribs, deep narrow slots, or internal features that do not contribute to strength, assembly, or heat transfer.
- Use generous corner radii instead of sharp re-entrant corners.
- Apply tight tolerances only to mating or functional surfaces.
- Consider an open profile with a separate cover or insert when an enclosed cavity is not essential.
- Leave enough material for a simple post-extrusion machining operation if that produces a much simpler die.

A feature moved from the die to a secondary operation is not automatically a saving. It must be priced across the expected quantity. Suppose a simplified die costs $1,000 less but adds $1.50 of machining per part. At 100 pieces, the added machining costs $150, leaving an $850 net saving. At 500 pieces, the saving falls to $250. At 1,000 pieces, the machining costs $1,500 and the supposedly cheaper design becomes $500 more expensive.

That calculation illustrates the central discipline of short run costing: never remove a tooling expense without pricing the operation that replaces it.

## Search existing tooling before commissioning a new die

The fastest way to reduce fixed cost is often to avoid paying for a completely new die. A supplier may already have a die with a compatible profile family, die block, circumscribing circle, or similar opening that can be modified for the new application.

A tooling audit should answer several specific questions:

- Does an existing die match the required press size and profile envelope?
- Can a slot, flange, or opening be modified without weakening the die?
- Is the die in good condition, or has wear already changed the bearing surfaces?
- Is the existing tool owned by the supplier or another customer?
- Can the modified die hold the required dimensions and surface quality?
- Will the tool remain useful for future orders?

For example, a new die might cost $2,000, while modifying a suitable existing die might cost $700. If both options require the same $500 press setup, the tooling-related cost is $2,500 for the new route and $1,200 for the modified route. On a 100-piece run, that difference is $13 per part. On a 500-piece run, it is $2.60 per part.

Even if the modified profile requires an extra $0.50 per part for adjustment or machining, the saving remains $12.50 per part at 100 pieces and $2.10 per part at 500 pieces.

Existing tooling is not automatically a bargain. A worn die can produce dimensional drift, poor surface quality, or excessive startup scrap. The right comparison is the total cost of an inspected, usable tool against the total cost of a new one—not the die invoice alone.

## Choose tooling based on cumulative demand

The first purchase order is not always the correct volume for a tooling decision. A product may need 100 pieces for validation but 2,000 pieces over the next year. Conversely, a prototype may never reach production after testing.

A durable custom die makes sense when its lower operating cost will repay the higher initial investment. A simplified or modified die makes sense when the design is still changing or future demand is uncertain.

The break-even calculation is straightforward:

`Break-even quantity = additional fixed cost ÷ per-part savings from the higher-cost tool`

Consider two illustrative options:

- Simplified tooling: $1,100 in fixed costs and $22.50 variable cost per part
- Durable custom tooling: $2,900 in fixed costs and $20.80 variable cost per part

The durable option requires $1,800 more up front but saves $1.70 per part. Its break-even point is about 1,060 pieces.

At 100 pieces, the simplified option costs $33.50 per part, while the durable option costs $49.80. At 1,500 pieces, the simplified option costs about $23.23 per part, while the durable option costs about $22.73.

The durable tool wins only after the project passes the break-even quantity. If a forecasted reorder is merely possible, it should not be treated as guaranteed volume. A useful planning approach is to calculate three cases:

1. No reorder after the first run
2. The most likely reorder quantity
3. The full optimistic demand forecast

If the durable die is justified only by the optimistic case, the lower-risk choice is usually a staged tooling plan: use a practical die for the pilot, freeze the design after testing, and invest in production-grade tooling when demand is demonstrated.

Die life also needs context. A tool rated for tens of thousands of kilograms has little economic value to a project that may consume only a few hundred kilograms and then change its design. The same tool can be a strong investment for a stable product with recurring orders.

## Press setup is a second fixed cost

Tooling economics do not end with the die invoice. Press setup, warm-up, trial material, and first-article approval can dominate very small orders.

A $450 setup charge adds $9 per part to a 50-piece order but only $0.90 per part to a 500-piece order. Two separate 50-piece runs would carry $18 per part in setup charges, while one 100-piece run would carry approximately $4.50 per part, assuming the supplier can produce the full quantity in one campaign.

This makes order timing important. When demand is predictable, combining releases can save more than negotiating a small reduction in aluminum price. The decision should include inventory risk, storage, cash flow, and the possibility that the design will change before the second release.

Scheduling can also reduce indirect setup waste. Grouping compatible jobs by alloy, finish, or press size may reduce cleaning, heating, and changeover time. For multiple profiles, each die may still require its own change, but an experienced production schedule can limit unnecessary downtime.

Startup scrap deserves attention as well. Two rejected pieces represent 4% scrap on a 50-piece run but only 0.4% on a 500-piece run. First-article approval, die correction, and process stabilization are therefore disproportionately expensive in a small batch.

## Make the supplier show the cost curve

A unit price by itself hides the economics that matter most. A useful request asks for prices at several quantities, such as 50, 100, 250, and 500 pieces, while separating fixed and variable charges.

The quote should identify:

- New die fabrication
- Existing die modification
- Die trial and correction allowance
- Press setup and minimum billet or run charge
- Aluminum price basis and expected yield
- Cutting and finishing minimums
- First-article inspection
- Secondary machining
- Packaging and freight
- Tool ownership, storage, and future reuse

The difference between the 50-piece and 500-piece quotes reveals how much of the project is fixed. If the unit price falls sharply as quantity rises, the supplier is spreading tooling, setup, or batch minimums across more parts. If it barely changes, the cost may be driven more by material weight, machining, or finishing.

A supplier that cannot separate these categories makes it difficult to decide whether a design change is worthwhile. A supplier that can show the fixed-cost and variable-cost components gives the buyer a way to test alternatives before committing to tooling.

## A practical decision rule

For a one-time order of 25 to 100 pieces, prioritize an existing or modified die, a simple cross-section, and minimal setup repetition. Paying slightly more for a secondary operation can be sensible if it avoids a large custom-tool investment.

For a 100- to 500-piece order with an unproven design, choose a profile that fits common tooling and use a staged investment. Avoid paying for a highly optimized production die before testing fit, performance, and customer acceptance.

For recurring demand that clearly exceeds the durable-tool break-even point, invest in a dedicated die designed for stable production. The additional upfront cost can reduce per-part conversion cost, correction risk, and future scheduling friction.

Short run aluminum extrusion becomes economical when fixed costs are engineered rather than merely negotiated. The winning profile is not always the lightest one, and the cheapest first die is not always the best lifetime choice. The sound decision is the one that minimizes die complexity, avoids repeated setup charges, and matches the tool investment to demand that can actually be supported by the product forecast.
