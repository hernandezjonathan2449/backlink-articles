# Sustainable Aluminum Verification: Proof Beyond Recycled Content

## Sustainable Aluminum Is a Claim, Not a Material Label

Aluminum's recyclability is a material characteristic. Sustainability of a particular extrusion is a documented claim about a supply chain. Those statements are related, but they are not interchangeable. A finished profile can look identical whether its billet came from a high-recycled-content loop or from mostly primary metal, and neither appearance nor alloy designation reveals the environmental burden.

In supplier qualification, the recurring mistake is treating recycled content as a verdict instead of one input to an evidence trail. A supplier can accurately report 80% recycled aluminum and still leave unanswered whether the percentage is pre-consumer or post-consumer, whether the remaining primary metal came from a low-carbon smelter, and whether the accounting applies to the delivered order. That distinction is the foundation of [verified sustainable aluminum](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/sustainable-aluminum-extrusions-from-greenwashing-to-verified-green_n996): the claim must be traceable, testable, and comparable.

## A Recycled-Content Percentage Is Only the First Data Point

Primary and recycled metal have materially different energy requirements. At the metal-production stage, commonly cited order-of-magnitude figures place primary aluminum around 14,000–16,000 kWh per metric ton, while remelting clean scrap may require roughly 700–800 kWh per metric ton. Those figures explain why recycled feedstock can reduce impact dramatically. They do not, by themselves, establish the footprint of a finished extrusion.

Under those illustrative values, shifting 10% of a metric ton from primary input to remelted scrap would reduce metal-stage energy by about 1,425 kWh before collection, sorting, alloy correction, casting losses, extrusion, heat treatment, coating, and transportation. The benefit is real, but a buyer still needs to know whether the claimed shift occurred in the material supplied.

A percentage alone leaves several important questions open:

- **What kind of recycled material is included?** Pre-consumer scrap may come from trimming, setup, or rejected material within a manufacturing system. Post-consumer material may come from demolished buildings, retired vehicles, or used packaging. Both can be legitimate inputs, but they represent different circularity outcomes.
- **Where is the primary portion from?** The carbon intensity of primary aluminum varies sharply with the electricity used for refining and smelting. Two products with the same recycled percentage can have very different cradle-to-gate emissions.
- **What does the percentage describe?** It may refer to billet input, a monthly facility average, a product family, or a specific shipment. Those are not equivalent claims.
- **How are losses handled?** Scrap collection, remelting yield, dross, alloy adjustment, and production returns affect how much input becomes saleable extrusion.
- **Is the claim physical or accounting-based?** A mass-balance system can allocate certified recycled attributes across a mixed stream. That can be credible when governed by clear rules, but it does not necessarily mean every profile contains physically segregated post-consumer atoms.

The right response is not to dismiss recycled content. It is to require a definition precise enough to audit. A claim of 60% recycled content should be accompanied by the basis of calculation, the pre-consumer and post-consumer split, the time period, and the material-accounting method.

## Four Links Turn a Sustainability Claim Into Evidence

A defensible claim connects four kinds of information. If one link is missing, the claim may still be directionally useful, but it is not strong enough for a procurement decision that depends on documented environmental performance.

### 1. Material Identity and Composition

The supplier should identify the alloy, billet or input category, and recycled content by mass. For an extrusion order, the useful record includes:

- total recycled content percentage;
- pre-consumer and post-consumer percentages separately;
- the source or source class of the recycled material;
- the production lot or accounting period covered;
- the treatment of internal process scrap and customer returns.

This level of detail prevents a facility-wide average from being presented as an order-specific fact. It also keeps alloy control visible. Mixed aluminum scrap can contain different alloying elements, coatings, sealants, or contaminants, so a credible recycling program must show how material is sorted and how chemistry is corrected before extrusion-grade billet is cast.

### 2. Energy Use and Carbon Boundary

A carbon number is useful only when its unit and boundary are clear. A supplier reporting 1.2 kilograms of CO2e per kilogram of extrusion is making a very different claim from one reporting 1.2 kilograms per kilogram for extrusion alone. The first may include upstream metal production, while the second may start at the plant gate.

A comparable disclosure should identify:

- the declared unit, such as kilograms of CO2e per kilogram of finished extrusion;
- whether the result is cradle-to-gate, gate-to-gate, or another boundary;
- whether it includes alumina refining, primary smelting, remelting, billet casting, extrusion, heat treatment, and surface finishing;
- the electricity data, emissions factors, and reporting year;
- which data are measured at the facility and which are industry averages.

For environmental product declarations, the A1–A3 modules commonly cover raw-material supply, transport to the manufacturer, and manufacturing. An A3-only figure should not be compared directly with an A1–A3 declaration. The same discipline applies to energy: kilowatt-hours per ton and kilograms of CO2e per ton are related but not interchangeable, because the electricity source changes the emissions factor.

### 3. Chain of Custody and Allocation

Chain-of-custody records answer a basic question: can the supplier reconcile what entered the system with what left it?

In a physically segregated model, certified material remains identified through defined stages. In a mass-balance model, certified and non-certified inputs may be processed together, but the accounting system limits how much certified output can be assigned to those inputs. Neither model should be accepted on a logo alone.

Look for input and output reconciliation, rules for internal scrap, controls against double counting, and a statement of whether the claim applies to a specific lot or a broader period. If a supplier cannot explain how recycled attributes move from billet purchases to finished profiles, the percentage is a marketing assertion rather than a traceable material claim.

### 4. Independent Assurance

Independent review gives the first three links credibility, but the certificate must be read for scope. The relevant questions are practical:

- Is the named legal entity the same company issuing the material?
- Does the certificate cover the actual extrusion and finishing facility?
- Are the processes used for the order within its scope?
- Is the certificate current, and who performed the audit?
- Does the document verify a management system, a material chain of custody, a product footprint, or some other claim?

The distinction between common credentials matters:

- **ISO 14001** demonstrates an environmental management system with processes for identifying impacts and pursuing continual improvement. It does not set a maximum carbon intensity or prove a particular recycled-content percentage.
- **ASI Performance Standard certification** addresses environmental, social, and governance practices across relevant aluminum operations. **ASI Chain of Custody certification** adds controls for responsible material accounting and transfers.
- **An environmental product declaration** presents lifecycle results under defined product-category rules and is typically independently verified. It is a transparent impact declaration, not a universal rating that a product is environmentally superior.

No single certificate answers every sustainability question. A management-system certificate, a chain-of-custody certificate, and a product footprint serve different purposes.

## Why a Company Certificate Is Not Automatically an Order Certificate

Procurement teams often collect a certificate and treat the sustainability review as complete. That shortcut fails when the certificate's scope is broader or narrower than the claim being purchased.

A corporate ISO 14001 certificate may cover a headquarters environmental system while excluding the plant making the profiles. A chain-of-custody certificate may cover certain material categories but not every alloy, finishing line, or accounting period. An EPD may describe a generic product family using average data rather than the electricity mix and recycled content of the shipment under consideration.

The certificate is still useful. It shows that a control framework exists or that a declared product has passed a defined verification process. The mistake is treating that framework as automatic proof for every output.

A strong review therefore matches three scopes:

1. **Entity scope:** the legal manufacturer and physical facility.
2. **Process scope:** billet production, extrusion, heat treatment, anodizing, powder coating, or other operations included in the claim.
3. **Product scope:** the alloy, profile family, recycled-content model, and carbon boundary used for the actual purchase.

If any of the three is unclear, the buyer should record the claim as unverified rather than filling the gap with assumptions.

## An Illustrative Supplier Comparison

Consider two bids for the same family of architectural profiles.

**Supplier A** offers 85% recycled content. The response does not split pre-consumer and post-consumer material, gives no carbon value in kilograms of CO2e, and attaches an ISO 14001 certificate for the corporate group. The supplier describes the product as low carbon but does not state the smelter source, accounting method, or boundary.

**Supplier B** offers 68% recycled content: 44% post-consumer and 24% pre-consumer. The remaining primary metal is identified by source category, the facility provides a cradle-to-gate footprint, and current chain-of-custody records cover the extrusion plant and the relevant material period.

Supplier B is not automatically lower-carbon simply because its documentation is better. Supplier A might have a lower footprint if its primary aluminum comes from a very low-carbon source and its manufacturing is efficient. But Supplier B is decision-ready: its claim can be compared, tested, and challenged. Supplier A's 85% figure cannot receive full sustainability credit until the missing evidence is supplied.

The preferred bid depends on the project objective. If post-consumer recovery is the priority, B has provided the more relevant fact. If embodied carbon is the priority, both suppliers must provide comparable footprint results. If both objectives matter, score them separately rather than allowing one attractive percentage to stand in for every environmental attribute.

## Put the Evidence Requirement Into the RFQ

Verification works best when it is a purchase requirement rather than an informal request made after a supplier has been selected. A useful specification can state:

> For each supplied alloy and production lot, the supplier shall report total recycled aluminum content by mass, with pre-consumer and post-consumer percentages stated separately. The supplier shall identify the chain-of-custody model, the accounting period, the applicable facility, and the evidence supporting the calculation.

Add the following requirements where carbon performance matters:

1. Provide a product carbon footprint in a stated unit, with the system boundary, reporting year, emissions factors, and allocation method.
2. Identify whether the result is product-specific, facility-average, or industry-average data.
3. Provide current certificates and declarations with certificate numbers, issuing bodies, facility scope, covered processes, and expiration dates.
4. Reconcile recycled inputs, production scrap, and certified outputs without double counting.
5. Require advance notice if the smelter, billet source, recycled-content mix, accounting model, or finishing route changes.
6. Make unsupported or out-of-scope claims a documentation nonconformance, not a marketing disagreement.

For identical profiles, kilograms of CO2e per kilogram can provide a useful comparison. When designs differ substantially, the evaluation should also consider the function delivered. A lighter profile that provides the same service may change the result, while a coating or treatment that materially extends service life may justify additional processing impacts. The boundary and functional basis must be stated before bids are ranked.

## Red Flags That Signal an Unverified Claim

Certain phrases are not proof by themselves:

- up to a stated recycled percentage;
- contains recycled material, without a mass percentage;
- low carbon, with no unit or emissions boundary;
- carbon neutral, with no treatment of residual emissions or offsets;
- certified sustainable, with only a logo and no certificate scope;
- 100% recyclable, presented as evidence of low production emissions.

A credible supplier is usually willing to disclose limitations. Useful signals include a dated declaration, a clear distinction between pre-consumer and post-consumer feedstock, facility-specific records, a named audit body, and an explanation of what the certificate does not cover. Transparency about uncertainty is stronger evidence than a perfect-sounding claim with no supporting data.

## The Decision Rule for Verified Green Aluminum

The most defensible supplier is not necessarily the one with the highest recycled-content number. It is the one that can answer four questions without changing definitions:

- What material entered the process?
- How was the material and its environmental attributes accounted for?
- What energy and emissions boundary produced the reported result?
- Who independently checked the claim, and did the check cover this facility and product?

Aluminum has genuine sustainability advantages, especially when recycled metal replaces primary production and when products remain in service long enough to avoid replacement. Those advantages become procurement-grade environmental value only when the claim is tied to a specific material stream, a transparent calculation, and an auditable record.

Verified green is therefore not a color, a slogan, or a recycled-content percentage. It is a result that another qualified party can trace and reproduce.
