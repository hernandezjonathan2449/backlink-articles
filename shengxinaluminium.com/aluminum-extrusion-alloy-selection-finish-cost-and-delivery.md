# Aluminum Extrusion Alloy Selection: Finish, Cost, and Delivery

## The alloy choice is the real starting point

In extrusion, the alloy line on the print does more than define chemistry. It decides how fast the profile can run, how clean the surface will look after anodizing, how much pressure the die must survive, and how much post-processing the part needs before it ships. A practical [alloy to delivery workflow](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-profile-extrusion-manufacturers-from-alloy-selection-to-delivery_n830) starts with one question: what does the finished profile actually need to do?

If that question is answered late, the project pays for it in scrap, die wear, rework, and missed dates. If it is answered early, the rest of the process becomes simpler. The right press, the right die geometry, the right temper, and the right finish all fall into place around the alloy instead of fighting it.

## Why alloy selection controls manufacturability

A profile is not difficult or easy in the abstract. It is difficult or easy in a specific alloy.

Lower-strength, more extrudable alloys move through the die with less pressure. That gives the manufacturer more room to hold wall thickness, maintain straightness, and keep the surface smooth. Harder alloys usually need slower ram speeds, more conservative die design, and more post-extrusion handling. The result is often a longer lead time and a higher chance of cosmetic marks or distortion.

That is why alloy choice changes more than mechanical performance:

- It affects whether thin walls can run cleanly or start to wave.
- It changes how much die wear the supplier will see over a production run.
- It influences anodizing color consistency and surface appearance.
- It can reduce or increase the amount of straightening, cutting, and machining needed after extrusion.
- It often determines whether the project can use a stock alloy or needs special material ordering.

The buyer who treats all aluminum profiles as interchangeable usually ends up paying for that assumption later.

## 6063 and 6061 solve different problems

The most common mistake is asking for the strongest alloy by default. Strength matters, but so does what the profile must look like, how it will be made, and what happens after extrusion.

### 6063 when surface quality and shape complexity matter

6063 is the architectural favorite for a reason. It extrudes cleanly, handles complex shapes well, and tends to give a better surface for anodizing and visible finishes. Window frames, door systems, handrails, LED housings, and decorative trim often perform better in 6063 than in a stronger alloy that is harder to push and less forgiving to finish.

The practical advantage is not just appearance. When a profile has tight cosmetic requirements, 6063 often reduces rejects because the alloy works with the process instead of against it. If the part will be seen, touched, and installed in long runs where color match matters, that matters more than a small increase in tensile strength.

### 6061 when load and machining drive the design

6061 is the alloy people reach for when the part has to carry weight, resist abuse, or survive extensive fabrication. It has a real strength advantage, and it machines well enough for brackets, frames, and structural sections that need drilled holes, tapped features, or CNC operations after extrusion.

The trade-off is equally real. 6061 is less forgiving in the press and usually less attractive on a cosmetic finish. If the profile is visible and anodized, the finish response is often not as clean as 6063. If the geometry is thin, deep, or highly detailed, 6061 can make the die work harder and the schedule move slower.

A lot of projects that start as 6061 should be 6063, and a lot of projects that start as 6063 should be 6061. The right answer depends on whether the part is mostly structural or mostly visual.

## 6005 and 6005A are often the smarter compromise

Not every profile needs the full jump to 6061. Not every decorative part should stay in 6063 if the structure is getting pushed hard. That is where 6005 and 6005A often earn their place.

These alloys sit in the middle in a practical sense. They can offer stronger performance than 6063 while staying easier to extrude than 6061 in many shapes. That combination makes them useful for transportation frames, modular structures, conveyor components, racks, and curtain wall members where the profile has to do more than look good but still needs to run efficiently.

From a production standpoint, that middle ground can save real money. Faster extrusion speeds, better weldability, and less die stress often translate into shorter lead times and fewer surprises during qualification. For repeated production, that can matter more than a slight difference in published strength numbers.

## 6463 exists for a reason

When appearance is the product, not an afterthought, 6463 can be the better call. It is often chosen for bright decorative anodizing, premium trim, and consumer-facing parts where color and reflectivity matter as much as dimensions.

In real projects, 6463 is rarely the cheapest path, but it can be the least expensive way to avoid cosmetic rejects. If the finished piece lives in a showroom, a retail space, or a premium architectural environment, the extra attention to finish quality may be worth more than an incremental cost saving on raw alloy.

## Temper is part of the alloy decision

Specifying the alloy without the temper leaves too much undone. 6063-T5 and 6063-T6 are not interchangeable in practice, even though they start from the same base metal. The same is true for 6061 and 6005.

T5 tempers usually fit profiles that are extruded and then artificially aged. They are common in architectural work because they balance strength, straightness, and production efficiency. T6 tempers go further on strength, but they also raise processing demands and can extend the route from press to finished part.

That matters when a project is already tight on schedule. If a drawing calls for unnecessary T6 performance, the part may spend more time in heat treatment, more time being straightened, and more time in the queue before shipment. If a profile does not need that extra strength, the temper choice can be a hidden source of delay.

## The wrong alloy creates three kinds of waste

The cost of a poor alloy decision does not stop at the material line.

**First, there is manufacturing waste.**  
Harder alloys can slow press speeds, reduce die life, and increase scrap. A profile that should have run smoothly may need more trials, more adjustment, and more inspection.

**Second, there is finishing waste.**  
If the part is visible, the wrong alloy can produce inconsistent anodizing, poor color match, or a surface that highlights die lines and minor defects.

**Third, there is design waste.**  
Over-specifying strength often leads to heavier profiles than needed. Under-specifying strength forces downstream reinforcement, brackets, or secondary machining that add cost after the die is already paid for.

Those costs show up differently on the quote, but they come from the same source: using alloy selection as a guess instead of a design decision.

## The questions that actually matter

The cleanest alloy choice usually comes from five questions:

- Will the profile be seen, painted, or anodized in a way customers will inspect closely?
- Is the part carrying load, resisting impact, or mainly serving as a frame or cover?
- Will it need welding, bending, or a lot of CNC machining after extrusion?
- Does the geometry have thin walls, deep hollows, or long unsupported spans?
- Is the project trying to minimize unit cost, tooling cost, or total lead time?

If the answers point toward appearance, 6063 or 6463 usually deserves the first look. If the answers point toward load and machining, 6061 is often justified. If the project needs a balance of the two, 6005 or 6005A frequently solves more problems than it creates.

## The best manufacturers start with alloy, not price

A capable extrusion partner does not begin by asking only how many tons are needed. The better conversation starts with end use, finish requirements, fabrication steps, and the profile's weak points. That is where a good manufacturer earns its keep: by recommending the alloy that can actually be produced well, not the one that merely sounds strongest on paper.

When the alloy is right, the rest of the project becomes easier to quote, easier to run, and easier to deliver on time. When the alloy is wrong, even the best press and the best die are forced to compensate for a bad starting point.

The most reliable extrusion programs do not treat alloy selection as a line item. They treat it as the foundation.
