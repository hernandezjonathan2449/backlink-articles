# Aluminum Extrusion Cost in Saudi Arabia: Beyond Price per Kilogram

## The real purchase is a performing system, not a pile of aluminum

A Saudi buyer can receive two quotes for the same nominal alloy and still be comparing different products. One quote may cover only press output by weight. Another may include a stable die, controlled temper, architectural finishing, machining, packaging, conformity records, and a delivery sequence tied to the construction schedule. The numbers look comparable because both are expressed in SAR per kilogram. The risks are not.

For projects exposed to summer temperatures above 45°C, intense ultraviolet radiation, wind-blown dust, or coastal salt, the economically correct question is not which supplier sells aluminum most cheaply. It is which compliant profile delivers the required performance at the lowest expected cost over its service life.

> The right award is the lowest risk-adjusted cost of a compliant, installed, performing profile—not the lowest raw-metal price.

That distinction changes how alloy, finish, thermal breaks, fabrication, logistics, and supplier capability should be evaluated. It also explains why a quote with a higher nominal price can be the more economical choice.

## Why price per kilogram breaks down

Price per kilogram remains useful as a diagnostic. A large difference may reveal a different alloy, profile mass, metal-price basis, conversion rate, or scope of work. It is not sufficient as the final award metric because aluminum is only one part of the delivered product.

A realistic cost model includes:

- Billet alloy, extrusion conversion, and heat treatment
- Die design, tooling, trials, and first-article approval
- Anodizing, powder coating, PVDF, or another specified finish
- Cutting, drilling, CNC machining, threading, deburring, and assembly
- Protective packaging, inland transportation, freight, customs, and storage
- Dimensional inspection, material certificates, coating tests, and conformity documentation
- Rework, rejected parts, replacement shipments, and installation disruption
- Cooling energy, cleaning, recoating, maintenance, and eventual replacement

A useful working formula is:

**Expected project cost =** quoted conversion cost + tooling + finishing + fabrication + landed logistics + compliance + expected rework + schedule impact + operating and maintenance cost.

Not every item needs to appear on the supplier's invoice, but every item belongs in the buyer's comparison. A quote that excludes machining is not cheaper than a quote that includes machining; it is simply incomplete.

Weight can also distort the comparison. One supplier may reduce wall thickness and offer a lower mass per meter. That can be a genuine efficiency improvement if the section still meets load, deflection, tolerance, and durability requirements. If the lighter section needs additional brackets, produces more rejects, or requires redesign after testing, the material saving disappears.

## Alloy and geometry: pay for the required duty

Alloy selection affects more than mechanical strength. It influences extrusion speed, die life, surface quality, machining behavior, heat treatment, and the ability to produce a complicated hollow or thin-wall shape consistently.

6063 is widely used for architectural profiles because it extrudes smoothly and produces attractive anodized or painted surfaces. Typical published tensile values for 6063-T6 are around 35 ksi, depending on the applicable product standard and section. 6061-T6 is commonly closer to 45 ksi and offers stronger structural and machining performance. 6082 can provide still higher strength within the 6000 series, but it is more challenging for intricate thin-wall profiles and may deliver a less refined extruded surface.

Those differences create two opposite procurement risks:

- Using 6061 or 6082 where 6063 meets the design can add unnecessary conversion difficulty, finishing risk, and cost.
- Using 6063 where the component requires higher strength can lead to excessive wall thickness, deflection, redesign, or failure.

A high-rise façade illustrates the problem. A decorative cover, pressure plate, and structural mullion may sit next to one another, but they do not perform the same function. The architect may prioritize surface quality on one profile while the engineer prioritizes strength, deflection, or connection capacity on another. Treating every component as the same alloy priced by weight hides those requirements.

Profile geometry matters just as much. Unbalanced walls, narrow ribs, sharp corners, and complex hollows place greater demands on die design and press control. A supplier with the right press range and tooling expertise may charge more for conversion but achieve faster sample approval and a lower scrap rate.

The commercial questions should therefore include:

- What alloy and temper are included, and what certificate proves them?
- What is the finished mass per meter and the allowable dimensional tolerance?
- Can the supplier produce the profile on a suitable press without compromising wall balance?
- Are die corrections, trials, and first-article samples included?
- Does the quoted price assume one production run or several smaller batches?

A lower mass per meter is valuable only when the profile still performs the job for which it was designed.

## Finish price is not exposure cost

In Saudi Arabia, surface finishing is a climate specification rather than a decorative afterthought. Riyadh brings intense UV exposure, heat cycling, and abrasive dust. Jeddah, the Red Sea coast, and parts of the Eastern Province add salt-laden humidity. Industrial areas may expose the finish to chemical contaminants that do not exist in a residential compound.

A mill finish may be suitable for a concealed indoor component, but it is rarely an appropriate default for an exposed architectural façade. The relevant comparison is not the cost of applying a finish; it is the cost of preserving appearance and corrosion protection for the required service life.

Anodizing creates an oxide layer integrated with the aluminum. It provides strong scratch resistance and does not peel like an external paint film, which can be valuable where sand and frequent contact are concerns. Its color range is more limited, and anodized color consistency between production lots requires careful control.

Powder coating provides broad color and texture options at a generally lower initial cost than premium liquid systems. Its weakness is localized damage: an impact or deep scratch can expose the underlying metal. Pretreatment, film thickness, curing, and the selected powder formulation determine whether the finish performs well in a high-UV environment.

PVDF systems are often selected for major exterior façades, coastal developments, and buildings where long-term color and chalk resistance justify a higher initial cost. Product warranties and weathering performance depend on the specific resin, pretreatment, film build, application process, and exposure category. A generic statement that a coating is suitable for exterior use is not enough.

A serious finish comparison should identify:

- The coating or anodizing class and minimum thickness
- Pretreatment chemistry and curing requirements
- Color, gloss, texture, and acceptable batch variation
- Relevant weathering, adhesion, and corrosion test evidence
- Touch-up procedures and whether field repair will match the factory finish
- Cleaning frequency and the access equipment required for maintenance

For example, ASTM B117 salt-spray results can help evaluate corrosion resistance, but the test duration alone does not predict every real-world façade outcome. The supplier should also provide the applicable coating standard, accelerated weathering evidence, and a warranty tied to the actual project environment.

A cheaper finish can become expensive when damaged profiles need to be replaced after installation, when a visible color mismatch forces a whole elevation to be recoated, or when access equipment is needed years earlier than planned.

## Thermal breaks turn an upfront premium into an operating decision

A thermally broken aluminum frame is not simply an aluminum profile with an insulating strip added to the quote. Its performance depends on the polyamide width, crimping quality, inner and outer profile geometry, seals, glazing, spacers, and installation details.

Common systems use reinforced polyamide strips that physically separate the interior and exterior aluminum sections. Because polyamide conducts heat far less efficiently than aluminum, the break reduces the direct thermal bridge through the frame. High-quality systems may achieve frame Uf values around 0.8 to 1.5 W/m²K, compared with roughly 5.8 to 7.0 W/m²K for some non-thermally broken frames. Actual performance must come from the tested assembly rather than the strip width alone.

A simple screening calculation shows why the premium should be modeled instead of guessed. Assume a 5,000 m² glazed façade with 15 percent frame area. That gives 750 m² of aluminum frame. If a non-thermal frame has a Uf of 6.0 W/m²K and a thermal system has a Uf of 1.2 W/m²K, the difference is 4.8 W/m²K.

Using an illustrative cooling exposure of 2,000 hours at a 12 K temperature difference:

750 m² × 4.8 W/m²K × 12 K × 2,000 hours = 86,400 kWh of reduced thermal cooling load.

That is heat removed by the HVAC system, not automatically electricity saved. At an average cooling COP of 3, the electrical equivalent would be approximately 28,800 kWh. At an assumed commercial tariff of SAR 0.20/kWh, the energy value would be about SAR 5,760 per year before demand charges. If the thermal-break premium were SAR 100,000, the energy-only payback in this particular example would be about 17 years.

A larger façade, longer operating hours, higher temperature differences, demand charges, or a more efficient building model can change the result substantially. Solar heat gain, glazing, air leakage, and HVAC controls also matter. A claimed 20 to 30 percent reduction in window-related heat transfer should never be presented as a 20 to 30 percent reduction in whole-building electricity use.

The procurement lesson is not that thermal breaks always pay back quickly. It is that they should be priced against the building's operating model. They are often highly valuable in glazed offices, hotels, hospitals, and high-specification developments, while a non-conditioned warehouse or interior partition may not justify the same premium.

Require frame Uf, complete window Uw, air and water performance, condensation analysis, and the exact glazing and gasket configuration. Without those details, two thermal-break quotes are not comparable.

## Fabrication, quality, and schedule determine installed cost

An extruded bar is rarely the final product installed on a Saudi project. It may still need to be cut, drilled, slotted, mitered, threaded, deburred, labeled, assembled, and protected during shipment. Every handoff introduces another opportunity for dimensional drift, scratches, incorrect hole locations, or conflicting tolerances.

The cost of quality becomes visible when a batch fails at installation. Consider 10,000 finished components with a 1.5 percent reject rate. That produces 150 rejected pieces. If each finished piece represents SAR 400 in fabrication, shipping, and installation effort, the direct rework exposure is SAR 60,000 before removal, access equipment, resequencing, or project delay.

Schedule losses can be just as material. In an illustrative case, 12 installers costing SAR 350 per person per day lose 10 working days because replacement profiles are late. The direct idle-labor cost is SAR 42,000, excluding cranes, supervision, storage, liquidated damages, and the effect on following trades. A quote that saves SAR 30,000 on aluminum has not created savings if it causes that delay.

An integrated supplier can reduce these risks by controlling die development, extrusion, finishing, CNC work, inspection, and packaging under one quality system. Integration is not automatically proof of capability, however. The buyer should confirm which operations are performed in-house, how subcontracted work is controlled, and who owns the corrective action when a finished component fails.

Capacity also needs a more precise definition. Annual tonnage does not guarantee an available production slot for a project's required dates. Ask for the proposed sample date, first-article approval date, weekly production capacity, finishing capacity, packaging method, and contingency plan for a delayed die or rejected batch.

Compliance documentation belongs in the same cost calculation. Material certificates, temper records, dimensional reports, coating records, traceability, and applicable Saudi or Gulf conformity documents can determine whether a shipment is released or held. Missing paperwork may create inspection, storage, re-testing, or replacement costs even when the metal itself meets specification.

## Normalize two bids before choosing them

The following example is an illustration of the comparison method, not a market quotation. Assume a 25,000 kg order for finished aluminum components.

Bid A appears attractive at SAR 3.20/kg, or SAR 80,000. The quote covers extrusion only. After adding SAR 12,000 for tooling and first-article work, SAR 17,500 for finishing, SAR 15,000 for cutting and CNC work, and SAR 10,000 for packaging and freight, the known delivered cost becomes SAR 134,500. If the process creates a SAR 20,000 rework exposure, the risk-adjusted cost reaches SAR 154,500.

Bid B is quoted at SAR 4.40/kg, or SAR 110,000. It includes the same alloy and finish performance, tooling, machining, packaging, local delivery, inspection records, and conformity documents. Its nominal kilogram price is 37.5 percent higher, but its normalized delivered cost is lower before any schedule or rework advantage is counted.

Bid B is not automatically better simply because it is bundled. Its scope and evidence still need verification. The point is that comparing SAR 3.20/kg with SAR 4.40/kg without normalizing the scope produces a false conclusion about value.

For imported material, the comparison should also include the metal-price basis, billet premium, currency, Incoterms, customs, Saudi conformity costs, inland transportation, storage, and the cost of carrying additional inventory. For local material, it should include delivery to the actual jobsite, capacity allocation, replacement response, and any outsourced finishing.

## Write the RFQ around performance

Before requesting proposals, use a [Saudi extrusion buying guide](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-saudi-arabia-what-buyers-must-know-before-ordering_n984) to map the local supply landscape, standards, and project questions. The RFQ must then convert that context into one consistent technical and commercial scope.

A performance-normalized request should include:

1. **Exposure and service life:** Identify the city, coastal or inland conditions, industrial contaminants, indoor or outdoor use, design life, cleaning plan, and expected UV or salt exposure.
2. **Engineering duty:** State the approved alloy and temper or provide the required strength, deflection, corrosion, and machinability criteria. Define wall tolerances, profile mass, connection loads, and allowable dimensional variation.
3. **Thermal and finish requirements:** Specify frame and assembly performance, coating or anodizing class, thickness, color tolerance, gloss, weathering evidence, corrosion testing, and warranty requirements.
4. **Complete process scope:** Require separate prices for die work, samples, extrusion, heat treatment, finishing, cutting, CNC machining, assembly, packaging, inspection, delivery, and documentation.
5. **Evidence and acceptance:** Define the first-article approval process, material certificates, dimensional inspection, finish testing, traceability, and the remedy for nonconforming parts.
6. **Schedule and commercial basis:** State sample dates, production cadence, delivery batches, metal-price validity, currency, freight terms, minimum order quantities, die ownership, and replacement lead times.

Ask suppliers to return both a price per kilogram and a price per finished meter, piece, or square meter delivered to the project. The first number helps diagnose metal and conversion economics. The second number is closer to what the project actually buys.

## The decision rule is lowest risk-adjusted cost

Total cost of ownership does not mean selecting the most expensive alloy, the thickest wall, PVDF for every application, or the largest thermal break available. It means paying for the performance that the specific exposure and duty require, then avoiding costs that do not improve the result.

A covered interior profile may need little more than consistent dimensions and a durable basic finish. A coastal hotel façade may justify premium weathering protection, strict color control, and thermally broken assemblies because access, appearance, cooling demand, and replacement costs are high. A machinery frame may place greater value on 6061-T6 strength, CNC accuracy, and repeatability than on architectural anodizing.

During evaluation, ask one diagnostic question: **What was removed to make this quote cheaper?** The answer should identify a documented design efficiency, a genuine production improvement, or a different commercial assumption. If the only answer is a lower SAR-per-kilogram figure, the comparison is incomplete.

The most economical Saudi aluminum extrusion is the one that reaches the site on schedule, passes acceptance, performs in its environment, and does not force the project to pay for the same component twice.
