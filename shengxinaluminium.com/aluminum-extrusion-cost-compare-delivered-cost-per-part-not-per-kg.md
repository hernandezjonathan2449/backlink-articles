# Aluminum Extrusion Cost: Compare Delivered Cost Per Part, Not Per Kg

## The Real Unit of Aluminum Extrusion Cost Is the Usable Part

The most common error in an aluminum extrusion quote review happens when kilograms are treated as the product. They are not. Kilograms measure metal; the purchase is a conforming profile at a specified length, finish, tolerance, packaging method, and destination.

Two suppliers can quote honestly at different per-kilogram rates and still deliver opposite economic outcomes. One may have a higher metal-plus-conversion rate but lower tooling, finishing, machining, or freight costs. The other may advertise a cheaper rate while leaving important charges outside the headline number.

A practical rule follows: **per kilogram is a diagnostic unit; delivered cost per accepted part is the purchasing unit.** That broader view is the basis of a [delivered extrusion cost](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-cost-decoded-from-ingot-price-to-per-part-quote_n1046) comparison.

## Build One Cost Model Before Comparing Quotes

A normalized estimate can be written as:

**Per-part cost = (Wb × R) + F + M + P + (D + S + T) / Q**

The terms mean:

- **Wb:** billed aluminum weight per part, including any cut allowance or stock-length allocation.
- **R:** the weight-based rate. It may contain the metal index, regional premium, billet premium, and extrusion conversion fee.
- **F:** finishing per part, such as anodizing, powder coating, or chemical treatment.
- **M:** cutting, drilling, tapping, CNC work, deburring, inspection, or assembly per part.
- **P:** packaging per part.
- **D:** die or tooling cost.
- **S:** setup, trial, testing, or short-run charges.
- **T:** lot-level freight, insurance, duties, brokerage, and other delivery charges.
- **Q:** the number of parts to which those fixed or lot-level charges apply.

The formula looks simple, but the denominators are not always the same. A die may be amortized over a committed annual volume, a setup charge may apply to every press run, and freight may apply to every shipment. Treating all three as one fixed cost divided by annual demand can produce a misleadingly low result.

For a true cost per accepted part, divide the total cost by the number of conforming parts received, not merely the number ordered. If expected yield is 98%, the economic denominator is approximately 0.98 times the ordered quantity. Do not add a separate yield penalty if the supplier has already included expected scrap in the quoted weight or conversion rate.

Keep the components visible even when the final calculation uses one combined rate. The metal index and regional premium move with the market; conversion, finishing, and fabrication usually follow different cost drivers. Combining them too early makes later price changes difficult to explain.

## Why a Lower Per-Kg Rate Can Still Cost More

### Fixed charges are diluted by quantity, not by kilograms

A custom die costs the same whether the first order produces 100 parts or 10,000 parts. The press setup, sample run, inspection plan, and production release also carry fixed effort. A supplier can therefore offer a low conversion rate and still be more expensive on a short run.

Tooling creates a second distinction between cash cost and normalized cost. A $1,200 die allocated over 1,000 pieces contributes $1.20 per part to the comparison, but the buyer still needs to pay the full $1,200 on the initial invoice. A sound sourcing decision tracks both the upfront cash requirement and the amortized cost.

### Finishing follows surface area and batch behavior

Anodizing and powder coating do not inherently follow metal weight. A finned heat sink can expose a large surface area while weighing little. A thick solid bar can weigh much more while presenting relatively little surface area. Finishers may price by surface area, rack load, linear length, batch, minimum lot, or weight, depending on the process and equipment.

That makes a per-kilogram comparison especially weak for thin-wall, high-perimeter profiles. If two profiles weigh 1.5 kilograms per meter but one has twice the surface area, the finishing burden may be very different even though the metal line is identical.

### Fabrication follows features and part count

A hole, tapped thread, slot, end mill, or deburring operation is usually performed once per part or once per feature. It does not become proportionally cheaper because the extrusion weighs more.

For example, an illustrative drilling charge of $0.65 per piece adds $2,600 to a 4,000-piece order. A competing extrusion rate that is $0.15 per kilogram lower saves only $600 if each part weighs one kilogram. The machining line outweighs the apparent material-rate advantage.

This is why a quote must state whether cutting and machining are included. A supplier quoting only extrusion may appear cheaper than one quoting finished parts, even when the finished-part invoice would reverse the ranking.

### Logistics follows length, volume, and delivery terms

Long profiles are not always heavy enough to make freight a minor cost. Protective film, interleaving, crates, pallets, and the unused space around long bundles can determine freight cost. Carriers may price the shipment by linear length, cube, handling class, or minimum charge rather than by aluminum weight alone.

An ex-works quote and a delivered-to-dock quote are not comparable numbers. The buyer may need to add inland freight, export handling, customs brokerage, duties, insurance, and unloading to the ex-works option. If taxes are recoverable, keep them outside both comparisons; if duties and brokerage are buyer costs, include them in landed cost.

### Conversion is not a pure commodity charge

The extrusion conversion fee reflects press time, setup, alloy, die complexity, speed, process yield, stretching, cutting, and heat treatment. A light profile with difficult metal flow may consume more press time per kilogram than a heavier, easy-running profile.

A low conversion rate is valuable only when it refers to the same profile, alloy, temper, tolerances, and production assumptions. Otherwise, it may simply reflect a different scope of work.

## A Worked Quote Comparison

Consider 1,000 finished parts with the same 6063-T5 profile, 1.50 kilograms of billed weight per part, anodizing, cut-and-drill work, and delivery to the same receiving location. The figures below are illustrative, but the comparison shows how a lower per-kilogram rate can lose on total cost.

**Supplier A has the higher weight-based rate:**

- Metal plus conversion: $4.40 per kilogram × 1.50 kilograms = **$6.60 per part**
- Die allocation: $1,200 ÷ 1,000 = **$1.20 per part**
- Anodizing: **$0.85 per part**
- Cutting and drilling: **$1.10 per part**
- Packaging: **$0.20 per part**
- Freight: **$0.45 per part**
- **Delivered total: $10.40 per part**

**Supplier B has the lower weight-based rate:**

- Metal plus conversion: $3.95 per kilogram × 1.50 kilograms = **$5.93 per part**
- Die allocation: $1,900 ÷ 1,000 = **$1.90 per part**
- Anodizing: **$1.20 per part**
- Cutting and drilling: **$0.95 per part**
- Packaging: **$0.35 per part**
- Freight: **$0.80 per part**
- **Delivered total: $11.13 per part**

Supplier B looks $0.45 per kilogram cheaper. Supplier A is actually about $0.725 cheaper per finished part, saving roughly $725 across the order. The difference comes from tooling, finishing, packaging, and freight, not from an error in the metal arithmetic.

The comparison assumes identical technical scope, quality, delivery terms, and pricing date. If Supplier B offers a shorter lead time or better documented quality, those factors may justify the premium. The key point is that the per-kilogram headline cannot make that decision by itself.

## Quantity and Release Pattern Change the Answer

Suppose a project has a $1,200 die, a $400 setup charge per press run, and $600 freight per shipment. Look only at the fixed and lot-level portion:

- **200 parts in one run:** ($1,200 + $400 + $600) ÷ 200 = **$11.00 per part**
- **2,000 parts in one run:** ($1,200 + $400 + $600) ÷ 2,000 = **$1.10 per part**
- **2,000 parts in ten runs:** ($1,200 + $4,000 in setups + $6,000 in freight) ÷ 2,000 = **$5.60 per part**

The annual demand is the same in the last two cases, but the economic result is not. Ten small releases repeat the setup and freight burden. A blanket order may reduce that burden only if the supplier actually produces the full quantity in an efficient run and stores or releases it under agreed terms.

A quote request should therefore state both total annual volume and the expected release schedule. Ask whether the die is charged once, whether setup repeats per profile or per release, whether freight is charged per shipment, and whether multiple profiles can share a run or packaging lot.

## The Billed Weight Is Rarely the CAD Weight

The nominal part weight is often calculated as profile weight per meter multiplied by finished cut length. The supplier may bill something different because the process consumes material before and after the finished part.

Suppose the profile weighs 1.50 kilograms per meter and the finished cut is one meter. The nominal weight is 1.50 kilograms. If the supplier bills 1.03 meters to cover end trim, saw loss, and the agreed cutoff allowance, the billed weight becomes 1.545 kilograms. At a weight-based rate of $4.40 per kilogram, that allowance adds about $0.20 per part, or approximately $198 on 1,000 pieces.

The same issue appears when parts are cut from fixed mill lengths. Six one-meter pieces sound like an exact fit in a six-meter bar until saw kerf and end trim are included. The cutting plan may require a longer stock length, produce a remnant, or yield only five saleable pieces from a nominal six-meter length.

Ask whether the supplier bills theoretical part weight, actual shipped weight, full stock lengths, or a defined cutoff allowance. The correct model uses the weight that will actually be charged.

## Separate Market Risk From Supplier Conversion

Metal exposure should be transparent even when the final price is delivered per part. Request the metal index, regional premium, billet basis, reference date, conversion fee, and quote validity period.

For example, if the metal basis rises by $0.20 per kilogram and the billed part weight is 1.50 kilograms, the direct metal impact is $0.30 per part. If the total quote rises by $0.90 per part while conversion and finishing are supposed to be fixed, the remaining $0.60 needs an explanation. It may come from a premium, currency movement, freight, or a changed scope, but it should not be hidden inside an unexplained all-in rate.

Per-kilogram pricing is useful for measuring this exposure. It is also useful when comparing the same profile, finish, quantity, and delivery scope across suppliers. It becomes unreliable when the quote packages different services under the same unit.

## The Same Method Decides Between Stock and Custom Profiles

The stock-versus-custom decision also depends on delivered cost, not just the catalog rate.

Assume a stock profile costs $8.50 per delivered part and a custom profile costs $7.30 per part before an $1,800 die. If the operations and freight scope are otherwise equal, the custom option reaches break-even at:

**$1,800 ÷ ($8.50 - $7.30) = 1,500 parts**

At 500 parts:

- Stock: 500 × $8.50 = **$4,250**
- Custom: 500 × $7.30 + $1,800 = **$5,450**

At 3,000 parts:

- Stock: 3,000 × $8.50 = **$25,500**
- Custom: 3,000 × $7.30 + $1,800 = **$23,700**

The stock option wins at low volume, while the custom option wins after the fixed tooling cost has been diluted. If the stock profile also requires extra machining or connectors, add those charges before calculating the break-even point.

## Design Changes Should Be Tested at Break-Even

Profile design decisions should be evaluated as changes to the entire delivered-cost stack. Reducing wall thickness may lower metal cost but increase press difficulty, scrap, or distortion. Adding an integrated boss or groove may increase die cost while eliminating a machining operation on every part.

For example, suppose an integrated feature adds $800 to the die but removes $0.45 of machining per part. The feature breaks even at:

**$800 ÷ $0.45 = approximately 1,778 parts**

At 500 parts, the machining savings are only $225. At 5,000 parts, the savings reach $2,250 before accounting for any change in press speed or yield. If the feature also slows extrusion, the conversion impact must be added to the break-even calculation.

The same logic applies to tolerances, alloy selection, standard cut lengths, and finish requirements. The best design is not necessarily the one with the lowest metal weight. It is the one that lowers the total cost of producing an accepted part without creating a larger downstream charge.

## Normalize Every Quote Before Choosing a Supplier

An explicit [per-part quote framework](https://www.shengxinaluminium.com/aluminum-extrusion-cost-decoded-from-ingot-price-to-per-part-quote_n1046) keeps the comparison focused on the costs that reach the receiving dock. A disciplined review should cover these points:

1. **Set the commercial basis.** Compare the same currency, delivery point, Incoterm, tax treatment, customs responsibility, and quote validity period.
2. **Freeze the technical scope.** Confirm alloy, temper, profile drawing, cut length, tolerances, finish, color, coating thickness, machining, inspection, and certificates.
3. **Separate the rate layers.** Show the metal index, regional and billet premiums, conversion fee, die, setup, finish, fabrication, packaging, and freight separately.
4. **Verify the billed weight.** Ask how cutoff allowance, saw kerf, full stock lengths, remnants, and scrap are handled.
5. **Use the real release schedule.** Apply die, setup, packaging, and freight charges to the quantity and frequency that will actually occur.
6. **Calculate accepted-part cost.** Include expected yield, incoming rejection, replacement handling, and any quality-related charges without double counting supplier assumptions.
7. **Run sensitivity checks.** Recalculate the result for a metal-index change, a smaller release, an extra shipment, or a different tooling amortization volume.

## Use Per Kilogram as a Diagnostic, Not a Decision Rule

Three cost views can be useful at the same time:

- **Per kilogram** shows metal exposure and helps track market-linked changes.
- **Per meter** helps compare continuous profiles, cut plans, and long-length logistics.
- **Per accepted part delivered** determines what the buyer actually pays for a usable component.

The first number explains part of the invoice. The third number determines whether the quote fits the project budget. A supplier with the lowest per-kilogram rate may still lose once tooling, surface area, machining, release frequency, and freight are normalized.

> The lowest per-kilogram extrusion quote is not necessarily the lowest-cost extrusion. Choose the quote that produces a conforming part at the lowest delivered cost over the volume and release pattern actually required.
